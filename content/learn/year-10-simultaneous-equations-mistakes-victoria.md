Simultaneous equations sit right at the heart of Year 10 maths under the Victorian Curriculum 2.0. They show up in algebra topics, in modelling problems and, before long, in VCE Maths Methods. For many students, this is the first topic where the method they choose matters as much as the algebra they do with it.

The good news is that the mistakes students make are predictable. Once you know what they are, they are very fixable.

## Why this topic trips students up

Simultaneous equations require students to hold two ideas in their head at once: two equations, two unknowns, and a method that connects them. It is a bigger cognitive step than solving a single equation, and the working is longer, so there are more places for things to go wrong before the final answer appears.

Students who are still shaky on linear equations or negative numbers will feel that shakiness amplified here. Students who are confident with algebra sometimes rush the method and make careless errors they would not make on shorter problems.

## Mistake 1: choosing the wrong method and sticking with it

Victorian Curriculum 2.0 expects students to use substitution, elimination and graphical methods. Each one suits different equation types, and picking the wrong one does not make the problem impossible, but it does make it much messier.

Substitution works cleanly when one equation already has a variable isolated (for example, y = 3x + 1). Elimination works cleanly when the coefficients of one variable match or are easy to match. Graphing is usually reserved for estimation or when the equations are already in a form that is easy to plot.

A common pattern: students default to substitution for everything. When neither equation has a variable isolated, the substitution step involves a messy rearrangement and that is where errors creep in. Learning to look at the equations first and choose the method deliberately is a skill in itself.

## Mistake 2: sign errors in elimination

This is the single most common source of lost marks. When students add or subtract equations to eliminate a variable, a negative sign in the wrong place changes everything.

The specific trap: when subtracting one equation from another, students often subtract the first term on the right-hand side but forget to subtract the second. So instead of subtracting the whole equation, they only subtract part of it.

Encourage your child to write out the step explicitly, lining up like terms vertically before adding or subtracting. It takes an extra ten seconds and it catches the error before it happens.

## Mistake 3: solving for one variable and stopping

Students find x, feel satisfied and write down x = 4 as their answer. But the question asked for both x and y.

This mistake happens partly because the relief of finding one value breaks the flow. It also happens in tests when time pressure kicks in. The fix is a habit: circle the question before starting and check at the end that the answer includes every variable the question asked for.

## Mistake 4: not checking the solution in both equations

A correct pair of values will satisfy both equations. Substituting back takes about thirty seconds and catches arithmetic errors that are otherwise invisible.

Most students know this. Very few actually do it. If your child's teacher has told them to check and they still lose marks on this topic, the checking step is probably the first thing worth reinforcing.

## Mistake 5: losing track of which substitution goes where

In substitution problems with longer expressions, students sometimes substitute back into the equation they rearranged rather than the other one. The result looks valid but is usually circular. When checking, the two equations give the same value because the student is effectively using the same relationship twice.

The rule is simple: after finding one variable, substitute back into whichever original equation looks simpler to work with.

## What about graphing?

Graphing simultaneous equations is covered in Year 10 and sometimes assessed directly, particularly in modelling contexts. The marks students lose here are usually about accuracy: sketching lines by hand without plotting enough points, or misreading the intersection from a rough graph.

If your child is asked to solve graphically, they should plot at least two clearly calculated points per line and state the intersection as a coordinate pair with both values, not just where the lines appear to cross.

## For students who already find this straightforward

If simultaneous equations feel comfortable, that is a great position to be in before VCE. The extension move is to work with non-linear simultaneous equations, where a straight line and a parabola intersect. This requires substitution (you cannot use elimination), gives two solutions rather than one and is a genuine preview of Year 11 Maths Methods content.

Pushing into word problems is also worth the time. Setting up two equations from a worded scenario, before any solving happens, is a skill that separates confident students from outstanding ones and it is exactly what assessments test at the higher end.

## What parents can do right now

Look at your child's most recent maths test or homework on this topic. Find the questions where marks were lost and try to categorise the error: was it method choice, a sign error, a missing variable or a checking step they skipped? Each type has a different fix.

If the working shows the right approach but arithmetic errors at the elimination step, that is different from working that shows the wrong method was used throughout. Targeted practice on the specific mistake closes gaps far more efficiently than redoing the whole topic from scratch.
