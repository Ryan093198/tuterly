*VCE General Mathematics replaced Further Mathematics from 2023. This guide uses the current name.*


Unit 3 and 4 General Mathematics is the version that counts toward the ATAR. Everything in Year 11 was preparation. This is the real thing, and the structure of the course changes enough that parents who helped their child navigate Units 1 and 2 often find Year 12 catches them off guard.

Here is what your child is actually working toward, and how to support them through it.

## What the course looks like in Year 12

VCE General Mathematics Units 3 and 4 has two areas of study: Data analysis, probability and statistics, and Discrete mathematics. In practice, the content is organised into four topics, and every student studies all four:

- **Unit 3:** Data analysis, and Recursion and financial modelling.
- **Unit 4:** Matrices, and Networks and decision mathematics.

There are no optional modules. If you have read older advice about schools choosing application modules, that described the old Further Mathematics course. In General Mathematics, all of the content in Units 3 and 4 is examinable.

The course is technology-active throughout. In both exams students may use an approved device with numerical, graphical, symbolic, financial and statistical functionality (usually a CAS calculator), plus one scientific calculator and one bound reference, and a formula sheet is provided. Students who are not fluent with their calculator are at a genuine disadvantage.

## How the study score is made up

The study score comes from school-assessed coursework (SACs) across Units 3 and 4 and two end-of-year exams:

- **Unit 3 SACs: 24 per cent.** An application task, which is a guided investigation of a given data set with several variables, run over 4 to 6 hours across one to two weeks. It moves through three components of increasing complexity: data plots, summary statistics, and modelling linear associations or trends. There is also a modelling or problem-solving task on Recursion and financial modelling, run over 2 to 3 hours across one week.
- **Unit 4 SACs: 16 per cent.** Two modelling or problem-solving tasks, one on Matrices and one on Networks and decision mathematics, each run over 2 to 3 hours across one week.
- **Exam 1: 30 per cent.**
- **Exam 2: 30 per cent.**

Coursework makes up 40 per cent of the study score, so consistent effort through the year genuinely matters, not just the end-of-year exams.

## The two exams

Each exam has 15 minutes of reading time and 1 hour and 30 minutes of writing time, and both cover all four topics.

Exam 1 is multiple choice. It has 40 questions worth one mark each: 16 on Data analysis, and 8 each on Recursion and financial modelling, Matrices, and Networks and decision mathematics. It sounds manageable until you realise that forty questions across four topics leaves very little margin for slow working. Speed and accuracy together are what earn marks here.

Exam 2 is the written response paper. It has short-answer and extended-answer questions, including multi-stage questions, worth 60 marks in total: 24 for Data analysis and 12 each for the other three topics. Students need to show working clearly and answer in full. Partial marks are available, which means a correct method with an arithmetic slip still earns something.

## Data analysis: the topic most students underestimate

Data analysis is the biggest single topic in both exams and is also the focus of the Unit 3 application task. It covers data distributions (including the normal model and z-scores), associations between two variables, least squares regression and residual analysis, data transformation, and time series. The vocabulary is specific and the exam questions are precise about it.

Students often lose marks here not because they cannot do the calculations, but because they write imprecise interpretations. Saying "as x increases, y increases" is not enough. The exam expects students to reference the variables by name, include numerical values from the context, and use language like "on average" when interpreting a regression line. Practising written interpretation is just as important as practising the calculations.

## Recursion and financial modelling: where the rules are strict

This topic rewards students who learn the recurrence relation notation early and use it consistently. It covers depreciation, compound interest investments and loans, reducing balance loans, annuities and perpetuities, and investments with regular additions. The concepts connect to real life, which helps, but the notation needs to be accurate or marks disappear quickly.

A common mistake is confusing the direction of a sequence or setting up a financial calculation incorrectly on the calculator. Exam questions here are worth going through carefully because the style of questioning repeats.

## Matrices and Networks: the Unit 4 topics

Matrices covers matrix operations and their applications, including transition matrices for modelling how populations or states change over time. Networks and decision mathematics covers graphs and networks, Eulerian and Hamiltonian paths, minimum spanning trees, flow problems, shortest paths, matching problems, and scheduling with critical path analysis.

Because these are taught in Unit 4, they have less time to settle before the exams than the Unit 3 topics. Together they account for 16 of the 40 questions in Exam 1 and 24 of the 60 marks in Exam 2, so students who are solid on Data analysis and Recursion but underprepared here will feel the gap.

## The multiple-choice exam: a different skill set

Exam 1 rewards a specific approach. Students who attempt questions in order and spend equal time on each one tend to run out of time. A better strategy is to move through the paper quickly on a first pass, answer the certain ones, mark the uncertain ones, and return. Every question is worth one mark.

Students should also know when to use their calculator and when not to. Some questions are faster done mentally. Others need the CAS. Recognising which is which comes from doing timed practice, not just working through questions untimed.

## For students aiming for a strong result

The students who do best are typically not the ones who know more content. They are the ones who make fewer errors under pressure and who know the exam format so well that nothing surprises them. That means doing past exams under timed, exam-like conditions from at least Term 3.

Use the right papers. VCAA publishes sample exams for General Mathematics and past exams from 2023 onwards, and these match the current format. Older Further Mathematics papers were built around a different structure, with optional modules, so they are only useful for selected questions on overlapping content.

## What parents can actually do

You do not need to know the mathematics to be useful here. Checking that your child is working through past exams rather than just re-reading notes makes a real difference. Ask to see their exam practice scores and whether they are reviewing errors or just counting them.

It is also worth asking when each SAC is scheduled, since coursework is 40 per cent of the study score and the dates come round quickly.

Keep an eye on calculator fluency too. If your child is still hunting for functions mid-practice exam, that is worth addressing well before the end-of-year exams.

## Where to go from here

If your child is struggling with a particular topic, or if they are aiming for a study score that opens specific pathways, a tutor who knows the VCE General Mathematics course well can target exactly what needs work. Ask how they will show you what has been covered and where your child's gaps are, so you are not guessing whether the tutoring is actually helping, and make sure the practice is built from VCAA exam-style questions.
