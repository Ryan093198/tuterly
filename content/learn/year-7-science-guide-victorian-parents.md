Year 7 science is one of the bigger subject shocks of the secondary transition. In primary school, science is often woven into general topics, a bit of weather here, some animal habitats there. In Year 7, Victorian schools shift to the Australian Curriculum's distinct science strands, and suddenly your child is expected to think like a biologist in one unit and a chemist in the next. That structural change trips up a lot of capable kids in the first term.

This guide covers what your child is actually studying, where students typically come unstuck, and what you can do to help at home, whether your child is struggling to keep up or ready to go further.

## What the Victorian curriculum covers in Year 7 science

Victorian schools follow the Australian Curriculum: Science, which at Year 7 organises content into four main areas. Biological sciences cover living things, cells, and classification. Chemical sciences introduce particles, mixtures, and separating substances. Physical sciences look at forces and energy. Earth and space sciences cover geological time, the rock cycle, and Earth's place in the solar system.

Alongside the content strands, students are assessed on Science Inquiry Skills: forming hypotheses, designing experiments, collecting data, and drawing evidence-based conclusions. This second layer catches many students out, because being able to recall facts about cells is a different skill from writing a valid method or interpreting a graph.

## Where students struggle most

The jump to discipline-specific vocabulary is the first obstacle. Words like "permeable," "solute," and "sedimentary" appear without much prior context, and if a student misses a key definition early in a unit, the rest of the content becomes hard to follow. Reading the textbook actively, rather than passively, matters more in science than in most subjects.

The other common sticking point is practical reports. Most Year 7 students have never written a formal scientific method or discussion before. Schools expect a specific structure, aim, hypothesis, method, results, discussion, conclusion, and many students lose marks not because they did the experiment wrong but because they did not know how to write it up.

For students finding the content itself hard, it often traces back to specific misunderstood concepts rather than a general science weakness. Particle theory, for example, is a foundational idea that underpins both chemistry and physics in later years. A shaky understanding at Year 7 creates compounding problems in Years 8 and 9.

## What to do if your child is behind

Start by looking at their assessment tasks and teacher feedback, not just the mark. A comment like "needs to justify conclusions with evidence" tells you exactly what to work on.

For content gaps, watching short explainer videos on specific topics can work well because Year 7 science covers concrete, visual concepts. But videos alone do not build the exam and report-writing skills. Your child needs to practise writing responses, not just recognising correct answers.

If the practical report structure is the issue, work through one example report together. Ask them to explain what each section is for. Students who understand the purpose of a discussion, to explain why results support or contradict the hypothesis, write better ones than students who are just filling in a template.

## What to do if your child is ready for more

Year 7 is also a very good time to extend strong science students before the content gets harder. Encouraging genuine curiosity about the topics works well at this age. Following an interest in astronomy or chemistry beyond the classroom, through science documentaries, museum visits, or competitions like Science Talent Search, builds the habits of a student who does well in senior science.

Extension at this level also means pushing on the inquiry skills. A student who can already plan a fair test and explain their reasoning has a significant advantage when Year 9 and 10 science becomes more independently assessed.

## How to stay across what your child is learning

Ask your child which unit they are currently studying rather than how science is going in general. A specific question gets a specific answer. If they can explain the difference between a mixture and a compound, they have understood that unit. If they cannot, that is a useful signal.

Science notebooks and assessment feedback are worth looking at each term. The transition to secondary school means parents often see less of what is happening academically, but that information is still there if you ask for it.
