## First, the perspective you need

The 2026 NAPLAN individual student reports reached Victorian families in July, and for a lot of parents the result was not what they expected. If that is you, start here: NAPLAN is one test, sat on one morning, months ago. It is a snapshot, not a verdict on your child's ability or their future.

It is also not nothing. Used properly, it is a useful early-warning system, and the whole value of getting it in July is that there is time to act before it matters.

## What the levels actually signal

NAPLAN reports four proficiency levels: **Exceeding**, **Strong**, **Developing**, and **Needs additional support**.

The two that warrant attention are the bottom two, and it is worth being precise about why. **Developing** means your child has some of the expected skills but not all of them. **Needs additional support** means they are working meaningfully below the expected standard for that point in time.

The reason these matter more in numeracy and reading than elsewhere is compounding. Maths in particular is cumulative: Year 5 fractions feed Year 7 ratios, which feed Year 9 algebra, which is what VCE Methods assumes is automatic. A gap left alone does not stay the same size, it widens, because the curriculum keeps moving.

The reassuring flip side is that gaps found early are usually small and specific. It is rarely "bad at maths". It is more often three or four sub-skills that never quite landed.

## Do this before anything else: find out which skills

The single most common mistake is reacting to the band and not the detail. "Developing in numeracy" is not actionable. "Struggles to convert between fractions, decimals and percentages, and loses marks on multi-step worded problems" is.

Two ways to get that detail:

1. **Read the sub-area breakdown on the report.** It shows how your child went across the strands, not just an overall level.
2. **Ask the teacher.** A short email works: *"I saw the NAPLAN result. Which two or three specific skills would you focus on if you were me?"* Teachers usually give a far more precise answer than the report does, because they see your child every day.

## Then diagnose it yourself

The report is months old. Before you act on it, find out where your child is *now*. Sit them down with a practice test on the topic in question, mark it together, and sort every mistake into one of three buckets:

- **Silly mistake.** Knew the method, slipped. Not a knowledge gap.
- **Method gap.** Did not know how to start. This is the real target.
- **Ran out of time.** Knew it, too slow. Needs practice at speed, not re-teaching.

That twenty-minute exercise usually tells you more than the NAPLAN report did, because it is current and specific.

## What actually closes the gap

Once you know the two or three real gaps, the fix is not mysterious. It is targeted practice, spaced out, with someone checking the work.

- **Practise the specific gaps, not the whole subject.** Re-teaching everything wastes the effort your child does have in them.
- **Use mixed-topic questions.** School worksheets are sorted by topic, so your child knows the method before reading the question. Tests are not, and neither is NAPLAN. This is where a lot of the marks go.
- **Space it out.** Twenty minutes twice a week beats two hours on a Sunday, by a wide margin.
- **Mark it properly every time.** The marking is where the learning happens.

## When to get help

Consider a tutor if any of these are true: the gap is in numeracy or reading and sits at Developing or below; you have tried at home and it is creating conflict rather than progress; or your child has decided they are "bad at maths", because that belief does more long-term damage than the gap itself.
