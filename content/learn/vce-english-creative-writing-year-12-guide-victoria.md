The VCE English end-of-year exam has three sections, and most families spend their energy on the essay and the analysing argument task. Section C, the creative writing task, quietly catches students off guard. It carries real marks, it rewards a specific kind of preparation, and "just write something" is about the worst strategy you can bring to it.

Here is what your child actually needs to know before November.

## What Section C asks students to do

The creative writing task gives students a written prompt and asks them to produce an original piece of writing. The prompt is not a topic to write about directly. It is a stimulus: a phrase, an image description, or a short piece of text that students are meant to respond to creatively. The task is deliberately open, and that openness is where underprepared students stall.

Students have around 45 minutes for this section. They are expected to demonstrate control of language, a clear sense of voice, and deliberate craft choices. VCAA assessors are not looking for a complete short story with a tidy resolution. They are looking for writing that shows intent.

## What assessors are actually rewarding

This is the part most students misunderstand. The marks are not primarily for having a good idea. They are for executing that idea with skill. Assessors want to see:

- A distinct, consistent narrative voice
- Deliberate structural choices, not just writing from start to finish
- Language that is precise and purposeful, not decorative
- A response that genuinely engages with the prompt rather than ignoring it

A student who writes a technically polished 400 words will outscore a student who writes 700 muddled ones. Quality over quantity is not a cliché here, it is the actual marking logic.

## Why students underestimate this task

Creative writing feels less learnable than essay writing. Students assume it either comes naturally or it does not. That assumption costs marks.

The truth is that the skills assessed in Section C are teachable and improvable. Students who score well have almost always practised responding to unseen prompts under timed conditions, studied mentor texts, and received feedback on their craft choices specifically. They have also built a small toolkit of structural approaches so they are not starting from scratch in the exam room.

For extension students aiming at the top score bands, this is often where the gap opens. Strong analytical writers who have not developed their creative voice leave marks on the table that the essay will not recover.

## Practical ways to build these skills in Term 3

**Read like a writer.** Short fiction and personal essays are useful here, not because students should copy them, but because reading with attention to how a piece is constructed builds an instinct for craft. Ask: what is this writer doing, and why?

**Practise with real prompts.** VCAA releases past exam prompts. Working through them under timed conditions, even once a week, builds the mental muscle of responding quickly and with intention. Sitting down cold on exam day to write creatively for the first time under pressure is a very difficult position to be in.

**Get specific feedback.** General comments like "this is good" or "needs more detail" do not help a student improve their creative writing. Feedback needs to be specific to craft: voice, structure, word choice, how well the piece engages with the prompt. This is where a subject-specialist tutor makes a real difference compared to general revision.

## What parents can do

Your child's school will have covered the requirements, but Term 3 SACs and exam prep often mean Section C gets less class time than the other tasks. Ask your child directly: have they practised creative writing under timed conditions? Have they got feedback that explained what to improve?

If the answer is vague or no, that is worth addressing now. November is close enough that targeted practice in the next eight weeks will move the needle, but not so close that there is nothing left to gain.
