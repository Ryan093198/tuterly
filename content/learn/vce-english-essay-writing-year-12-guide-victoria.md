## Why the essay is make or break in VCE English

Across both SACs and the end-of-year exam, written expression is where marks are won and lost. For Year 12 students in Victoria, the ability to write a clear, sustained argument about a text is not just one skill among many. It is the skill the VCAA is testing, over and over, in different forms.

Many students arrive at Year 12 with a general sense of how essays work. Few arrive knowing how to write one at the standard the examiners are actually looking for. There is a significant gap between "finishing the essay" and "constructing a persuasive, well-evidenced argument," and that gap costs students study scores they could have had.

## What VCAA examiners actually want to see

The VCAA examiners reports are published each year, and they say the same things repeatedly. Examiners want a clear contention, sustained across the whole piece. They want analysis of how the author constructs meaning, not just what happens in the text. And they want evidence used purposefully, not dropped in as decoration.

What they consistently flag as problems: essays that retell the plot, paragraphs that list techniques without explaining their effect, and conclusions that simply repeat the introduction word for word. These are not small stylistic issues. They are structural problems that cap a student's score regardless of how well they write at the sentence level.

## Building a thesis that actually argues something

The thesis is where most essays succeed or fail before the student has written a second sentence. A strong VCE English thesis takes a clear position on the text and signals the argument the student is going to develop. It is not a statement of fact. It is not a description of what the author wrote. It is a claim about how and why meaning is made.

A weak thesis might read: "In this novel, the author explores themes of identity and belonging." A stronger version makes an argument: "Through fragmented structure and unreliable narration, the author positions the reader to experience displacement as a condition that cannot be resolved, only endured."

The second version gives the essay somewhere to go. Every paragraph that follows can connect back to that central claim.

## How paragraphs should work

A common structure students are taught is TEEL (Topic, Evidence, Explanation, Link). It is a reasonable starting point, but students often use it mechanically without understanding what each element is actually doing.

The topic sentence should advance the argument, not just introduce a technique. The evidence should be specific and chosen because it does what the topic sentence claims. The explanation should analyse how the language or structure creates meaning, not just state that it does. The link should connect back to the contention, not simply repeat the topic sentence in different words.

Students who move through these steps quickly, without doing the analytical work in the middle, produce paragraphs that feel thin. Examiners notice. A single well-developed paragraph is worth more than three shallow ones.

## Using textual evidence without letting it take over

One of the clearest signs of a developing writer is over-quoting. Long block quotes followed by minimal commentary suggest the student is not confident enough in their own analysis to take up space with it.

Strong VCE essays use evidence selectively. Short, precise quotes embedded into the student's own sentences tend to work better than long extracts. The student's voice should dominate. Evidence supports the argument. It does not make it.

For students working on the text response, this means practising the skill of choosing the right quote, not just any quote. For students working on comparative essays, it means being able to move between two texts smoothly, using evidence from both to build a single coherent argument rather than running two separate analyses side by side.

## What parents can do right now

If your child is mid-SAC season, the most useful thing you can do is help them practise writing under realistic conditions. Timed essay practice, where they write a full response in the time allowed in the actual exam, builds the fluency that no amount of planning can replace.

Reading the essay together and asking simple questions is genuinely useful too. "What is this paragraph actually arguing?" "Can you show me where you explained the effect of that technique?" These questions prompt the same critical thinking the student needs to do independently in the exam room.

What does not help: rewriting sentences for them or telling them the essay is good when it is not. Honest, targeted feedback is uncomfortable but necessary at this stage of the year.
