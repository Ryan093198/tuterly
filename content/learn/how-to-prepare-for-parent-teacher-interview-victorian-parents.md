Parent-teacher interviews are ten minutes long, scheduled back-to-back, and over before you've remembered half of what you wanted to ask. Most Melbourne parents walk out with a vague sense of how their child is going rather than anything concrete enough to act on.

That's not a criticism of teachers. It's just the format. The parents who get the most out of these conversations come prepared.

## Before you go: do one thing

Ask your child what they find hard right now. Not what they dislike, what they find hard. Kids are often more honest about this than parents expect, and it gives you a specific thread to pull in the interview instead of relying on the teacher to surface everything.

Also pull out your child's most recent school report if you have one. Note any comment that felt vague or any result that surprised you. Those are your starting questions.

## Questions worth asking (not just polite ones)

Most parents ask some version of "how are they going?" Teachers will answer warmly, because they're kind people under time pressure. What you actually need is specific information.

Try these instead:

**On curriculum progress:** "Is my child working at, above, or below the expected level for this point in the year?" This is a direct question tied to the Victorian Curriculum, and a good teacher will be able to answer it plainly.

**On effort versus ability:** "Is there anything where they clearly understand the concept but aren't showing it in assessments?" This separates a skill gap from a test-taking or confidence issue, which need different responses.

**On what's coming:** "What's the next big topic or assessment, and is there anything I should be reinforcing at home before we get there?"

**On the hard stuff:** "Is there anything you'd want me to know that might not have come up in the report?" Teachers often have observations they don't put in writing. A direct, non-defensive question gives them room to share them.

## How to decode teacher language

Teachers are professionals who work with children every day and choose their words carefully. Some phrases have a specific meaning once you know what to listen for.

"Doing well" means your child is meeting expectations. It is not the same as excelling, and it's worth asking what that looks like in practice.

"Could push themselves a bit more" usually means the work is fine but the ceiling hasn't been tested. For a high-achieving student, this is worth digging into. Ask what extension opportunities exist within the class or whether your child is being given harder problems.

"Working hard" without mention of results sometimes signals that effort is strong but the skills aren't consolidating. Worth a follow-up question about which specific areas need more practice.

"We're keeping an eye on it" is a flag. It means something has been noticed but hasn't been addressed yet. Ask directly: what does that mean, and what should we be doing at home in the meantime?

## If the feedback reveals a gap

First, don't panic. A gap identified in Term 3 is fixable before the end of the year. The worst outcome is leaving the interview with vague reassurance and doing nothing.

Ask the teacher to name the specific skill or concept that's behind. "Maths" is not specific enough. "She's finding multi-step word problems in multiplication hard" is something you can actually work on.

Then ask what the school is already doing. You need to know whether you're supplementing a plan or filling a gap the school isn't actively addressing.

Once you know the specific skill, targeted practice at home or with a tutor can move things quickly. A few focused sessions on the actual gap beats general homework help every time.

## If your child is ahead of the curve

Parent-teacher interviews aren't just for catching problems. If your child is bored, finishing work early, or has stopped being challenged, that's worth raising too.

Ask about extension tasks or enrichment within the class. Ask whether your child is being differentiated for in the Victorian Curriculum. If the answer is vague, it might be worth exploring extension tutoring to keep the ceiling high and maintain the habit of being stretched.

## Following up after the interview

Send a short email within a day or two. Thank the teacher and confirm the one or two action points you took from the conversation. This is not about being the pushy parent. It creates a written record, keeps the conversation open, and shows the teacher you took it seriously.

Check in with your child after the interview too. You don't need to share everything, but letting them know you're working on the same things they are builds trust.
