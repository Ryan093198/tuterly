A disappointing maths test lands differently in every house. Some students hide the paper at the bottom of their bag. Some come home upset. Some shrug and say "I just don't get it". Parents can swing between worry and frustration, often in the same evening.

The good news is that a bad test is one of the most useful things a student can bring home, as long as it gets used properly. The paper is a precise map of what your child does not yet know. This guide walks through what to do in the first few days, how to turn the paper into a short practice plan, and how to tell whether things are improving.

## The first 24 hours: lower the temperature

Nothing useful happens while everyone is upset. Before anyone opens the test, it helps to agree on a few things.

**For parents:**

- Thank them for showing you. If students learn that bringing a test home leads to an argument, the next one stays in the bag.
- Ask questions before giving opinions. "Which part felt hardest?" gets a more honest answer than "What happened?"
- Separate the mark from the child. A low mark is information about some specific skills on one day. It is not a verdict on whether they are "a maths person".
- Hold off on big decisions such as changing classes or booking extra help until you have looked at what actually went wrong.

**For students:**

- Feeling flat after a bad result is normal. It does not mean you are bad at maths.
- Do not throw the paper out or leave it in your locker. It is the most useful study resource you have this term.
- One bad test rarely decides anything on its own. What you do in the next fortnight matters far more.

## Day two or three: read the paper properly

Once things are calmer, sit down with the test for about 20 to 30 minutes. If your child would rather do this alone first, that is fine. The aim is to understand the mistakes, not to relive them.

### Start with what went right

Look at the questions they got full marks on. This matters for confidence, and it also tells you where the foundations are solid. A Year 8 student who aced the percentages section but lost marks across algebra has a very different problem from one who lost a few marks everywhere.

### Then sort every lost mark

Go through each question where marks were lost and put it into one of four groups:

| Type of mistake | What it looks like | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Careless slip | Dropped a negative sign, copied a number wrongly, misread the question | Slower checking habits, not more teaching |
| Method gap | Did not know how to start, or used the wrong method | Re-learning the idea, then practising it |
| Partial understanding | Started well but went wrong partway through | Practising the step where it broke down |
| Time pressure | Knew how, but did not get to it or rushed it | Practice at test speed |

Be honest when sorting. Students often call a method gap a "silly mistake" because it feels better. A useful test: could they do a fresh version of the question now, without help? If not, it is not a silly mistake.

If you want a more detailed walk-through of this sorting step, our guide on [how to mark a practice maths test with your child](/learn/how-to-mark-a-practice-maths-test-with-your-child) goes further.

### Look for the pattern

When you have sorted the questions, step back. Most bad tests have a shape:

- **One topic dragged everything down.** Common in topic tests. Usually the most fixable situation.
- **The same kind of error keeps appearing across topics.** For example, negative numbers going wrong in algebra, equations and coordinate geometry. That points to a skill from an earlier year that needs repairing.
- **Mostly unanswered questions at the end.** That is a timing issue, which needs a different fix from a knowledge gap.
- **Errors everywhere with no clear pattern.** Sometimes a sign of anxiety on the day, sometimes a sign the student has been quietly lost for a while. Worth a conversation with the teacher.

## Turn the paper into a short practice list

This is the step most families skip. Reviewing the test feels productive, but understanding a worked solution is not the same as being able to do the question next week.

Pick the questions in the "method gap" and "partial understanding" groups. Usually there are between three and eight of them. These are the practice list.

For each one, the routine is simple:

1. **Try it again without looking at the solution.** Cover the teacher's corrections. Have a genuine go.
2. **Check against a worked solution.** Find exactly which step went wrong.
3. **Do a fresh question of the same type.** Not the same question again, which they will half remember. A new one with different numbers or a slightly different setting.
4. **Come back to it a few days later.** If they can still do a fresh version, it has stuck. If not, it goes back on the list.

That last step matters more than it looks. Getting a question right straight after studying the solution mostly tests short-term memory. Getting a new version right three or four days later is much better evidence that the skill is actually there. ## Talk to the teacher (briefly and early)

A short email to the maths teacher is often worth more than hours of guesswork at home. Keep it practical:

- Which topics should they focus on before the next assessment?
- Is there a chance to resubmit, correct the test or sit a redemption task? Some schools offer this, many do not.
- Is this result out of character compared with class work?

Teachers see your child do maths every week. They can usually tell you in two sentences whether this was a bad day or a pattern.

## Plan for the next test, not just this one

A bad mark on a topic test often reflects how the preparation went, not just what your child knows. Common patterns include:

- All the revision happened the night before.
- Revision meant re-reading notes and worked examples rather than doing questions.
- They only practised questions they could already do.

If any of those sound familiar, our parent guide on [preparing for maths topic tests](/parents/preparing-for-maths-topic-tests) sets out a simple fortnight-long plan. The short version: start practising about two weeks out, do questions rather than reading, and spend most of the time on the questions that went wrong.

## Signs things are getting better

Marks often lag behind progress, so look for earlier signals in the weeks after a bad test:

- Homework questions on the weak topic take less time and fewer prompts.
- Your child can explain how to start a question type that used to stump them.
- Their practice list is getting shorter rather than longer.
- They bring the next test home without being asked.

If after a few weeks of steady practice the same topics are still going wrong, that is the point to consider more support. Our comparison of [a maths app versus tutoring](/parents/maths-app-vs-tutoring) may help you decide what kind.

## When it is more than one bad test

One poor result is usually about specific skills. Several in a row, or a sudden drop after a strong start to the year, can have other causes: missed lessons, a change of class, friendship troubles, sleep, or anxiety about maths itself. If your child seems to dread maths rather than just dislike it, our article on [maths anxiety](/learn/maths-anxiety-help-children-victoria) is a good next read, and the school wellbeing team can help too.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I let my child see me looking at their bad test?

Yes, ideally together and calmly. Seeing you treat the paper as useful information, rather than as bad news, teaches them to do the same.

### How long should we spend reviewing a test?

About 20 to 30 minutes to sort the mistakes, then short practice sessions of 15 to 20 minutes spread across the next week or two. Long single sessions tend to end in frustration.

### Is it worth redoing the whole test?

Usually not. The questions they got right do not need practice. Focus on the ones in the "method gap" and "partial understanding" groups, using fresh versions of each question type.

### My child says the test was unfair. What should I do?

Listen first. Sometimes a test genuinely included content that was rushed in class. If so, raise it with the teacher politely. Either way, the practice list still applies: those topics will come back.

## Your next steps this week

1. Have a calm conversation and agree that the paper is useful, not shameful.
2. Sort every lost mark into slips, method gaps, partial understanding or time pressure.
3. Turn the method gaps into a short practice list of three to eight question types.
4. Practise fresh versions of each one, then check again a few days later.
5. Send the teacher a short, practical email.
6. Start preparing for the next test two weeks out, not the night before.
