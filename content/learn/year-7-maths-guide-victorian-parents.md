The jump from primary to secondary maths catches a lot of families off guard. Your child might have finished Year 6 in solid shape, then come home from the first few weeks of high school looking genuinely lost. That is not a sign something is wrong with them. It is a sign the curriculum has shifted in ways that nobody explained to you.

Here is what you actually need to know.

## What Year 7 maths covers in Victoria

Victorian Year 7 maths follows the Australian Curriculum v9.0, which all Victorian government and most Catholic schools now teach. The content is broader than most parents expect, and several topics appear formally for the first time.

The main strands your child will work through this year include:

- **Number:** integers (positive and negative), fractions, decimals, percentages, and the relationships between them
- **Algebra:** pronumerals, writing and evaluating expressions, and simple equations
- **Ratio and proportion:** introduced properly here for the first time, with real-world problem-solving
- **Geometry:** angle relationships, triangles, and classification of shapes with more formal reasoning
- **Statistics and probability:** collecting and interpreting data, calculating simple probabilities, and understanding chance on a 0 to 1 scale
- **Measurement:** area, perimeter, and volume of composite shapes

That is a lot of new territory. The pressure point is not any single topic. It is the pace at which new ideas arrive and the expectation that students will connect concepts rather than just follow a method.

## Where students actually get stuck

**Integers are the first wall.** Adding and subtracting negative numbers feels backwards when you first meet it. Many students learn a rule ("two negatives make a positive") without understanding why, and that falls apart the moment the question is worded differently. Spend time here. It underpins almost everything else in secondary maths.

**Algebra shifts the thinking required.** Primary maths is mostly about finding an answer. Algebra is about describing a relationship. That is a genuine conceptual shift, and students who rely on arithmetic intuition suddenly feel like the ground has moved.

**Ratio trips up capable students.** A child who is confident with fractions often still finds ratio confusing, because the notation and the way questions are worded feels unfamiliar. This is one of the most commonly under-practised topics in Year 7.

**The speed of delivery changes.** In primary school, a teacher might spend two weeks on one concept. In secondary school, a class might move through a topic in a few lessons. If your child misses a day, or needs more time to consolidate, the class moves on anyway.

## What is different about secondary school (and what parents miss)

In primary school, your child's teacher knew them well and could quietly adjust pace or re-explain something. In secondary school, your child is one of 25 to 30 students in a specialist maths class, and the expectation is that students advocate for themselves. Most 12 and 13 year olds do not do this naturally.

There is also less homework visibility. You may not realise your child has a gap until the first semester report lands. By then, the class is already several topics ahead.

Checking in briefly a couple of times a week, not to sit down and teach but just to ask which topic they are on and whether it makes sense, keeps you close enough to catch problems early.

## How to support your child at home

You do not need to remember how to solve equations to help. A few practical habits make a real difference.

Ask your child to explain a concept back to you in plain words. If they cannot do that, they probably cannot do it under exam pressure either. This is not a test. It is a low-stakes way to spot where the understanding is thin.

Encourage working through mistakes rather than avoiding them. In primary school, a wrong answer often just means try again. In secondary maths, understanding why an answer is wrong is usually where the actual learning happens.

For high performers, Year 7 is a good time to stretch beyond the curriculum. Competitions like the AMC (Australian Mathematics Competition) and school-based extension programmes often recruit in Year 7. Students who find the standard content comfortable benefit from harder problem-solving challenges, not just more of the same work.

## When to bring in a tutor

If your child comes home confused more than a couple of times in a row on the same topic, a tutor is likely more efficient than waiting. Gaps in Year 7 compound quickly. Integers and algebra underpin almost everything in Years 8 and 9, so getting those foundations right early pays off.

If your child is finding Year 7 straightforward, a tutor with an extension focus can move them ahead on algebraic thinking and problem-solving before those topics arrive in class. That kind of head start changes how confident a student feels in Years 9 and 10.
