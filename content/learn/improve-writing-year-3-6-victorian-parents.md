## Why writing is harder to help with than reading

Most parents feel comfortable listening to their child read and offering a bit of feedback. Writing is trickier. You can see that something is off, but pinpointing exactly what, or knowing how to fix it without crushing their confidence, is a different skill entirely.

The good news is you do not need to be an English teacher to make a real difference. You just need to know what your child is actually being asked to do at school, and how to support that at home.

## What the Australian Curriculum expects in Years 3 to 6

The Australian Curriculum (used in Victorian schools) organises writing around three main text types at primary level: narrative, persuasive, and informative writing. Each one has its own structure, language features, and purpose. Your child is not just learning to write, they are learning to write differently depending on what the task demands.

In Years 3 and 4, students are expected to plan and draft simple texts, organise ideas logically, and begin using paragraphs consistently. By Years 5 and 6, the expectations lift considerably. Students are assessed on their ability to structure arguments, vary sentence types, use evidence to support a point of view, and edit their own work for clarity and correctness.

This is a significant jump, and students who coast through Years 3 and 4 often hit a wall in Year 5 when the complexity increases.

## Narrative writing: story structure before story flair

Parents often assume narrative writing is the easiest type because children love telling stories. The problem is that a vivid imagination does not automatically produce a well-structured narrative on the page.

At the primary level, a strong narrative needs an orientation that sets the scene, a complication that creates tension, a sequence of events, and a resolution. Many children write long, rambling stories that never quite reach a point. Others write brief, underdeveloped pieces where nothing much happens.

A simple exercise at home: ask your child to tell you their story idea out loud before they write it. If they cannot describe what the problem is in their story and how it gets resolved, they are not ready to write it yet. Talking through structure before writing is exactly what teachers model in class, and it works.

## Persuasive writing: it is not just about opinions

Persuasive writing is where a lot of Year 5 and 6 students struggle most, especially students who are otherwise strong writers. The issue is that many children treat persuasive writing as a chance to share what they think. The curriculum expects something more structured than that.

A persuasive text needs a clear position stated up front, a series of reasons supported by evidence or examples, and a conclusion that reinforces the argument. Language choices matter too. Words like "clearly," "research shows," and "it is important that" are the kinds of connectives and persuasive devices teachers are looking for.

At home, dinner table debates are genuinely useful here. Ask your child to argue a position they do not personally hold. This builds the habit of constructing an argument rather than just expressing a feeling.

## Informative writing: teaching your child to organise knowledge

Informative texts, reports, explanations, procedural writing, are often treated as the dry option. But they are the text type your child will use most throughout secondary school and beyond.

The key skill here is organisation. Information needs to be grouped by topic, presented in a logical sequence, and written in a formal, objective tone. Primary students frequently mix personal opinion into informative texts ("I think dolphins are really cool") when they should be keeping the writing factual and structured.

A useful home practice: choose a topic your child is interested in and ask them to write five facts about it, grouped under two or three headings. This is low-stakes and builds the organisational habit that formal report writing requires.

## The editing habit: the skill most children skip

Across all three text types, the single most neglected skill in primary writing is editing. Most students write a draft, decide they are done, and hand it in.

Teach your child to read their work aloud before calling it finished. They will catch missing words, awkward sentences, and unclear ideas far more reliably by hearing the writing than by reading it silently. This is not a trick, it is genuinely how experienced writers work.

Start with just one focus per edit. Check for one thing at a time: full stops, then capital letters, then whether each sentence makes sense on its own. Trying to fix everything at once is overwhelming, and children give up quickly.

## Extension: what to do if your child is already a strong writer

If your child breezes through writing tasks and finds them genuinely easy, the goal is depth, not just length. Encourage them to experiment with more sophisticated sentence structures, vary how they open sentences, and think carefully about word choice rather than defaulting to the first word that comes to mind.

Reading widely is the most reliable way to build a stronger vocabulary and a more instinctive feel for how good writing works. Authors like Morris Gleitzman, Tristan Bancks, and Andy Griffiths write in ways that Year 5 and 6 students can both enjoy and learn from.

For students aiming well above year level, exploring the Year 7 writing expectations in the Australian Curriculum gives a clear picture of where strong primary writers are heading.
