## The honest answer

Yes, for most students, well-run online tutoring is as effective as in-person, and sometimes more so. The reason is simple: what makes tutoring work is a strong tutor, a clear plan, and consistent sessions with real feedback. None of those depend on being in the same room.

The catch is in "well-run". Online done badly, a distracted student, a weak connection, a tutor just talking at a screen, is worse than good in-person. So the question is less "does online work?" and more "what makes online work?"

## What makes it effective

**One-to-one attention.** The core advantage of tutoring, a session built entirely around your child, carries over online completely. A good tutor still adapts in real time to what your child does and does not understand.

**A shared whiteboard.** Effective online sessions are interactive, not a lecture. Tutor and student work through problems together on a shared digital whiteboard, with the student doing the work, not just watching.

**Access to the right tutor.** Because online removes the travel radius, your child can work with a genuine subject specialist rather than the nearest available option. For many families, this is the biggest single lift in quality.

**Structure around the session.** Reports after each lesson, targeted practice between sessions, and visible progress tracking turn isolated hours into a plan. This structure is often easier to deliver online, not harder.

## When in-person is still better

Online is not right for everyone. Very young children who cannot yet focus on a screen, or households without a reliable connection and a quiet space, may do better in person for now. And a small number of hands-on subjects genuinely benefit from being in the room. For mainstream academic tutoring, maths, English, the sciences, VCE, these are the exceptions rather than the rule.

## Before you book: try this for one week

Tutoring is usually one hour a week, and the slipping tends to happen in the six days in between. Before you commit to weekly sessions, it's worth trying a week of short, focused practice at home on maths, Years 7 to 10:

1. Each night, your child snaps the maths questions they got wrong.
2. They read the worked solution, then answer a new question on the same idea, not just the same question with new numbers.
3. At the end of the week, the weekly review tests them on up to 8 of that week's trickiest questions.

## How to give it the best chance

- Set up a quiet space and a device with a webcam.
- Have a way to show handwritten working, a stylus or a phone camera on paper.
- Choose a tutor who is comfortable teaching online, and who sends a report you can actually read after each session.
- Keep sessions consistent, weekly beats occasional.
