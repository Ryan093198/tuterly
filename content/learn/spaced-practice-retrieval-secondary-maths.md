Here is a familiar scene. There is a maths test on Thursday. On Wednesday night, after dinner, your child sits down with a worksheet of 30 questions on the topic. By 10pm they can do the questions. They feel ready. On Thursday, a question looks slightly different from the worksheet, and the method that felt solid twelve hours earlier has gone fuzzy.

That is not laziness or a lack of ability. It is how memory works. This guide explains, in plain language, why one big practice session fades so quickly, what works better, and how to build it into a normal school week without adding hours of extra study.

## Why the night-before worksheet feels like it works

When a student does 30 questions of the same type in a row, something useful does happen: they get faster and more accurate at that question type, in that session. The trouble is what is doing the work.

- **The method is still fresh.** By question five, the student is no longer working out how to start. They are repeating what they did in question four.
- **Every question is labelled.** On a worksheet titled "Solving linear equations", the student never has to decide which method to use. In a test, deciding is half the job.
- **Feeling fluent is not the same as remembering.** Being able to do something right now tells you very little about whether you will be able to do it in three days.

So the night-before worksheet produces a strong feeling of being ready, which is partly real and partly an illusion. Much of it fades within days, and a lot of it does not survive the slight changes in wording that tests always include.

## Two ideas that make practice stick

Learning research has looked at this question for a long time. Two findings come up again and again, and both are easy to use at home. We will describe them in plain terms rather than pile on references.

### Retrieval: pulling it out of memory

Retrieval practice means trying to recall or do something from memory, without looking at notes or a worked example first. It feels harder than re-reading, and that effort is the point. Each time a student successfully pulls a method out of memory, it becomes easier to pull out next time.

For maths, retrieval looks like:

- doing a question cold, before looking at the worked example
- covering the notes and writing down the steps for a question type
- doing a short mixed quiz on topics from earlier in the term

Re-reading notes and watching someone else solve a problem are not retrieval, which is why they feel easy and help less. Our article on [why practice tests beat re-reading notes](/learn/why-practice-tests-beat-re-reading-notes) covers this in more detail.

### Spacing: coming back after a gap

Spaced practice means spreading practice on a topic across several days or weeks instead of doing it all in one go. When a student comes back to a question type after a few days, they have partly forgotten it. Having to work to remember it is exactly what strengthens the memory.

The same total amount of practice, split into three short sessions across a week or two, generally produces better long-term results than one long session. It also feels less productive while you are doing it, which is why students rarely choose it on their own.

## The missing ingredient: practise what you got wrong

Spacing and retrieval tell you how to practise. They do not tell you what to practise. For secondary maths, the best answer is usually sitting in your child's exercise book and test papers: the questions they got wrong.

Mistakes are useful because they are specific. "I need to revise algebra" is vague. "I keep making errors when I expand brackets with a negative in front" is something you can practise in ten minutes. Our guide to [common Year 7 and 8 algebra mistakes](/learn/common-year-7-8-algebra-mistakes-and-how-to-fix-them) has plenty of examples of exactly this kind of error.

A practical way to combine all three ideas:

1. **Collect the questions your child gets wrong** in homework, class quizzes and tests.
2. **Retry each one from memory** a day or two later, before looking at the solution.
3. **Do a fresh version** of each question type, with different numbers or a slightly different setting, so they are practising the skill and not remembering the answer.
4. **Come back again** after several more days. If they can still do a fresh version, it is probably secure. If not, it stays on the list.

The key word in step 3 is "fresh". Redoing the exact same question works for a while, but students soon remember the answer instead of the method. New versions of the same idea keep the practice honest.

## A realistic weekly rhythm

None of this needs long study sessions. For most Years 7 to 10 students, 15 to 20 minutes on three or four days a week is enough to make a real difference, on top of normal homework.

| Day | What to do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Retry two or three questions from last week's mistakes, from memory | 10 to 15 minutes |
| Wednesday | Fresh versions of anything that went wrong on Monday, plus one or two new mistakes from this week | 15 minutes |
| Friday or the weekend | A short mixed set: a few questions from this topic and a few from earlier topics | 15 to 20 minutes |

Before a topic test, add a little more, but start about two weeks out rather than the night before. Our parent guide on [preparing for maths topic tests](/parents/preparing-for-maths-topic-tests) has a full fortnight plan, and [how many practice maths tests should my child do?](/learn/how-many-practice-maths-tests-should-my-child-do) covers how to space practice tests.

## Mixing topics: a useful extra

Once your child has a few topics under their belt, mixing question types in one practice session helps too. When questions on equations, percentages and area are shuffled together, students have to decide which method each question needs, just like in a test or exam. It is harder than doing a block of one type, and that difficulty is useful.

End-of-year exams are where this pays off most, because they cover a whole year of topics at once.

## What this looks like for parents

You do not need to be good at maths to help with any of this. The most useful things parents can do are about structure, not content:

- **Ask to see the mistakes, not the mark.** "Which questions went wrong?" leads somewhere. "What did you get?" usually does not.
- **Keep sessions short and regular.** Fifteen focused minutes on Monday, Wednesday and Saturday beats a two-hour Sunday session.
- **Ask them to explain a step.** If your child can explain how to start a question type out loud, they really know it.
- **Expect it to feel harder.** If practice feels easy, it might be too easy. A bit of struggle is a good sign.
- **Praise the habit.** Coming back to old mistakes is not glamorous. Notice when they do it.

## Tips for students

- Do the question before you look at the worked example. Even a wrong attempt helps you learn from the solution.
- Keep a list of the question types you get wrong. Your mistakes are your study plan.
- Come back to each one a few days later. If you can still do a new version, cross it off.
- Mix topics once you have a few. Tests do not tell you which method to use.
- Short and often beats long and once.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cramming ever useful?

A final look over key formulas the night before a test is fine. The problem is relying on one long session to learn the topic. Most of the practice should happen in the two weeks before.

### How long should the gaps between practice sessions be?

There is no single perfect gap. A practical approach is to come back to a question type after a day or two, then again after about a week. If it is still secure after that, revisit it occasionally before exams.

### Does this work for students who are already doing well?

Yes. Strong students often rely on being quick in class and then forget topics by exam time. Spaced practice from their own errors helps them keep earlier topics sharp, which matters most in end-of-year exams.

### What if my child does not make many mistakes?

Then use a short mixed quiz on earlier topics as the retrieval practice. Any question they hesitate on or get wrong becomes the next thing to practise.
