Year 5 is the year science quietly becomes harder to help with at the kitchen table. The topics shift from observation and sorting to genuine conceptual thinking, and plenty of parents who are perfectly comfortable helping with reading or times tables hit a wall when their child comes home asking about the water cycle or why metals conduct electricity.

You do not need a science degree to support your child through this year. But it helps to know what is actually on the curriculum and where the common sticking points are.

## What the Victorian curriculum covers in Year 5 science

Victorian schools follow the Australian Curriculum for science, structured into three strands: Biological Sciences, Chemical Sciences, Earth and Space Sciences, and Physical Sciences. At Year 5, the concepts in each strand take a step up in abstraction.

**Biological Sciences** focuses on how living things depend on each other and their environment. Students explore food chains and food webs, adaptations, and the relationships between producers, consumers, and decomposers. This is more than naming animals. Students are expected to explain *why* those relationships exist.

**Chemical Sciences** introduces the idea that materials have properties that can be observed and measured, and that some changes are reversible while others are not. Dissolving sugar in water is reversible. Burning paper is not. Year 5 students need to be able to classify changes and explain the difference.

**Earth and Space Sciences** covers the Earth's rotation and the patterns it creates, including day and night, seasons, and the movement of the sun across the sky. Students also look at how human activity affects the environment. This strand often surprises parents because it involves more reasoning than memorisation.

**Physical Sciences** looks at how forces, including gravity and friction, act on objects. Students explore how the size and direction of forces affect movement, and begin connecting cause and effect in more precise ways.

## Science inquiry: the skill your child is also being assessed on

Across all four strands, Year 5 students are also assessed on Science Inquiry Skills. This includes how they plan investigations, how they record and interpret data, and how they communicate their findings. A child who understands the content but cannot explain their reasoning clearly will still struggle on assessments.

Watch for whether your child can explain *why* they reached a conclusion, not just what the answer is. That habit of justification is built gradually and is worth practising at home even through everyday conversation.

## Where students typically get stuck

For students who are finding science difficult, the challenge is usually one of two things. Either the concept itself is abstract in a way that does not connect to anything familiar, or the scientific vocabulary is a barrier that makes the whole topic feel harder than it is.

"Reversible and irreversible changes" is a good example. The concept is straightforward once a child has a few solid examples they can picture and explain. Without those anchors, it becomes an empty phrase on a worksheet. Building up a small bank of real-world examples your child can visualise makes a noticeable difference.

For students who are already strong in science, Year 5 is a good time to push into the reasoning behind the facts. Ask them to explain what would happen if a variable changed, for instance, what if the Earth rotated in the opposite direction, or what if a food web lost its top predator. The Victorian curriculum does not cap what a curious student can explore.

## How to support your child at home without a science background

The good news is that Year 5 science lends itself well to conversation and observation rather than formal study sessions.

Talk through what they are covering at school and ask them to explain it to you in their own words. If they can teach it back, they understand it. If they stumble, that is useful information about where the gaps are.

Simple at-home experiments can reinforce the Chemical and Physical Sciences strands without any special equipment. Dissolving salt versus sand in water, testing which surfaces create more friction with a toy car, or tracking where shadows fall at different times of day all map directly to curriculum content.

For Earth and Space topics, the ABC Education website and the Australian Space Agency's education resources are both genuinely good and written for this age group.

## When a tutor makes sense

If your child is disengaged from science, consistently confused after class, or heading into Year 6 with shaky foundations, targeted support is worth considering. A good tutor will identify whether the issue is conceptual understanding, scientific vocabulary, or inquiry skills, because the fix is different for each.

For high-performing students, a tutor can introduce extension thinking that goes well beyond what class time allows, including more complex ecosystems, deeper physics concepts, or the kind of experimental design thinking that builds strong skills for secondary science.

A good primary science tutor understands the Victorian Curriculum and can tell whether the gap is understanding, vocabulary or inquiry skills.
