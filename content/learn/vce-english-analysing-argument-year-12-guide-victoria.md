## What is Analysing Argument, exactly?

In the VCE English end-of-year exam, Section C asks students to read one or more persuasive texts and write an analytical response. The task is not to share your child's opinion on the topic. It is to identify how the author is attempting to persuade their audience and explain how that works.

This is a genuinely different skill from writing a personal essay or an oral presentation. Many students spend most of Year 12 working on those forms and arrive at the exam underprepared for this one.

## Why students underestimate it

Section C is worth a significant portion of the exam, yet it often gets less preparation time than the other sections. Part of the reason is that it looks approachable on the surface. Students read the texts, they understand the argument, and they feel ready. The gap is in the analysis itself.

Strong Analysing Argument responses do not just identify techniques. They explain the effect those techniques have on a specific audience in a specific context. Naming a rhetorical question is not enough. Explaining why that question creates doubt in the mind of a reader who already holds a particular view is what earns marks.

## What the VCAA is actually looking for

According to the VCAA English Study Design, students are assessed on their ability to analyse how language and visual features are used to position readers. The key criteria that markers use include understanding of argument, analysis of language choices, and quality of written expression.

Notice that "understanding of argument" comes first. Before students can analyse how a text persuades, they need to accurately identify what position the author is taking and what the overall line of reasoning is. Students who misread the argument spend the whole response building on shaky ground.

## The two most common mistakes

The first mistake is technique-spotting without explanation. A list of identified features with no analysis of effect reads like a set of notes, not a response. Every technique observed needs to connect to the impact it creates.

The second mistake is ignoring context. Who published this piece? Where? Who is the intended reader? A column in a suburban community newspaper operates very differently from a national broadsheet opinion piece, and strong responses acknowledge that.

## How to actually improve

Reading widely helps more than most students expect. Exposure to opinion pieces, editorials, and letters in publications like The Age or ABC commentary trains students to spot argument structures quickly. This is not passive reading. It means reading with a question in mind: what is this writer doing, and why?

Practice under timed conditions matters too. Students often write excellent responses when they have unlimited time and can revise freely. The exam does not offer that. Writing a full Section C response in 45 to 50 minutes, regularly, is the only reliable way to build the speed and discipline the exam demands.

For extension students, the ceiling is not just a Band 6 score. It is a response that reads like genuine intellectual engagement with the text. That means choosing precise vocabulary, structuring paragraphs so each one advances a clear analytical point, and integrating quotation smoothly rather than dropping it in.

## Term 3 SACs are practice, not a ceiling

Many Victorian Year 12 students sit a practice Analysing Argument SAC in Term 3. The feedback from that task is some of the most useful information your child will receive before the October exam. If the feedback points to surface-level analysis or underdeveloped discussion of audience positioning, that is specific and fixable.

This is not the time to feel discouraged by a SAC result. It is the time to understand exactly what the feedback means and work on it deliberately in the weeks before the exam.

## Where parents can genuinely help

You do not need to be an English teacher to support your child through this. The most useful thing you can do is sit with them while they read an opinion piece and ask: what is this person trying to convince you of, and what are they doing to get there? That conversation, practiced regularly, builds the analytical instinct the exam rewards.

If your child's responses are technically competent but not scoring as well as expected, the gap is usually in the depth of analysis rather than the surface features. A tutor who knows the VCAA English criteria well can identify exactly where the thinking needs to go deeper.
