# VCE English: what makes a 9/10 essay (with structural breakdown)

Most students think a high-scoring VCE English essay comes down to impressive vocabulary or hitting a word count. It doesn't. Assessors are trained to look for three things: a sophisticated argument, well-integrated textual evidence, and controlled structure. Get those right and you're in the top band. Miss any one and you'll cap out around 7/10 no matter how many big words you use.

Here's what actually separates a strong essay from an excellent one.

## What a 9/10 essay looks like, structurally

**The opening doesn't restate the prompt.** A 7/10 essay typically opens by rephrasing the question and agreeing or disagreeing. A 9/10 essay opens with a contention that reveals how the student has interpreted the prompt. There's a difference between "I agree that Ransom explores the theme of grief" and "Malouf positions grief not as something to overcome, but as the condition through which his characters finally recognise each other's humanity." The second version shows the assessor you've done the thinking before you started writing.

**Body paragraphs build on each other.** In weaker essays, each paragraph makes a separate point that could be rearranged in any order. In a top-band essay, paragraph two deepens or complicates the argument from paragraph one. Think of it as layers, not a list. Each paragraph should make the previous one more interesting.

**The conclusion adds something.** If your conclusion could be copy-pasted from your introduction, it's not doing its job. A strong conclusion widens the lens: it connects your argument to a broader idea about the text, or acknowledges a tension your essay has surfaced. It shows the assessor you were still thinking at the end.

## The real differences between a 7 and a 9

**Embedded quotes vs bolt-on quotes.** A 7/10 essay drops a quote at the end of a sentence as proof. A 9/10 essay weaves the quote into the grammar of the sentence, making text and analysis feel inseparable. Compare: "This shows his fear. 'The darkness pressed in around him'" with "The way 'darkness pressed in' around the protagonist mirrors the psychological claustrophobia that defines his grief."

**Author's craft vs plot summary.** Assessors want to see you analyse what the author is doing and why, not retell what happens. Every time you write "this shows that," ask yourself: am I describing a plot event or a deliberate authorial choice?

**Nuanced contention vs binary response.** Prompts are designed to be arguable. Responding with a flat "yes, I agree" signals surface-level thinking. The best essays qualify their position: "While the text ultimately endorses reconciliation, it refuses to present forgiveness as painless."

## Common mistakes that hold essays back

Over-quoting is one of the most frequent. If your quotes are longer than your analysis, the ratio is wrong. Thesaurus abuse is another: replacing simple words with obscure synonyms doesn't read as sophisticated, it reads as uncertain. And perhaps the most costly mistake is ignoring the specific wording of the prompt. If the prompt says "to what extent," your essay needs to address degree, not just direction.
