## Why the oral presentation catches so many students off guard

The VCE English oral presentation is a formal SAC that contributes to your child's school-assessed coursework. It is not a bonus task or a warm-up. It is a graded piece of work under the current VCE English Study Design, and for many students, it lands in Term 3 right in the middle of the most pressured stretch of Year 12.

What makes it particularly stressful is that it asks students to do something most of them have never been explicitly taught. Standing up, speaking persuasively on a real-world issue, and doing it with enough polish to be assessed on structure, language, and delivery is a completely different skill from writing an essay. Yet most of the preparation advice students receive is vague. "Know your topic." "Practise in front of a mirror." That guidance rarely moves the needle.

## What the task actually requires

Under the VCE English Study Design, the oral presentation requires students to deliver a 4 to 6 minute persuasive piece on a contemporary issue. It is linked to their Reading and Creating work on argument and persuasive language. The key assessment criteria focus on the construction of a sustained, reasoned argument, the use of language appropriate to purpose and audience, and effective spoken delivery.

That last part trips students up constantly. Delivery is assessed, which means pace, tone, eye contact, and the ability to sound like a human being rather than someone reading a script at high speed are all part of the picture. A well-structured argument delivered nervously and at breakneck speed will cost marks.

## Choosing the right topic

Topic choice is where many students either set themselves up or create unnecessary difficulty. The issue needs to be genuinely contemporary and contestable. It cannot be purely hypothetical, nor can it be so broad that there is no clear position to argue.

A useful test: can your child clearly state what they want their audience to think or do after listening? If the answer is fuzzy, the topic is probably too vague. Issues connected to youth experience, technology, education, the environment, or public health tend to work well because students can speak with genuine understanding and find credible sources to support their arguments.

Students should avoid topics they feel neutral about. Persuasive speaking requires conviction, and audiences can tell the difference. Encourage your child to choose something they actually care about. That authenticity comes through.

## Structure matters more than most students realise

A strong oral presentation is not just a series of points loosely connected by "and also." It needs a clear through-line. The opening should hook the audience and establish the issue sharply. Each body section should build the argument forward, not just list separate ideas. The conclusion should land with genuine force, not trail off into summary.

Students working at the higher end of the grade range tend to use language that is deliberately chosen for impact. Rhetorical questions, repetition, direct address to the audience, and carefully constructed sentences all lift a presentation above the average. These are not tricks. They are the tools of persuasive language that students have been studying all year in texts by other writers.

This is a good moment to make the connection explicit. Ask your child to look back at the persuasive techniques they have analysed in the texts they have studied. The same toolkit applies here.

## Preparing for delivery

Rehearsal is non-negotiable, but not all rehearsal is equal. Reading the speech aloud once the night before is not preparation. It is wishful thinking.

Effective preparation means practising out loud multiple times, ideally in front of another person. It means timing the speech to land within the 4 to 6 minute window, because running under or over can affect the mark. It means identifying the two or three sentences that feel most unnatural and working on those specifically rather than running the whole thing from the top each time.

For students who freeze up under pressure, practising in low-stakes situations builds real confidence. A parent at the kitchen table, a sibling on the couch, or a trusted friend counts. The goal is to get the brain used to speaking the words while being watched, so it becomes less triggering when it counts.

## What parents can do right now

You do not need to be an English teacher to help. Your role in the lead-up to the SAC is to be the audience. Listen to the speech. Tell your child whether the argument is clear to someone coming in cold. Note whether they are making eye contact or reading directly from the page. Ask whether you were convinced.

If the answer to any of those is no, that is useful feedback. It is also much better to hear it from you at home than from an assessor during the SAC itself.
