Year 5 is a quieter year than most parents expect. There's no big exam looming, no school change on the immediate horizon. But mathematically, it's one of the most important years of primary school. The concepts introduced in Year 5 sit underneath everything that comes next: NAPLAN in Year 7, secondary school mathematics, and the confidence your child brings into Year 6 and beyond.

Getting a clear picture of what's expected now, and where the common sticking points are, puts you well ahead of any problems that might otherwise only surface at report time.

## What Year 5 maths actually covers in Victoria

The Victorian Curriculum F-10 sets out the content areas for Year 5 under three broad strands: Number and Algebra, Measurement and Geometry, and Statistics and Probability.

In practical terms, your child is expected to move confidently between whole numbers, fractions, decimals, and simple percentages. They're learning to multiply and divide larger numbers using written strategies, not just mental methods. They're introduced to the coordinate plane, work with 2D and 3D shapes, and begin calculating area and perimeter more formally. Data and chance round out the year.

That's a lot of ground. And for many students, two or three of those topics quietly fall behind while everything else keeps moving.

## The three areas where Year 5 students most commonly struggle

**Fractions and decimals.** These two topics arrive together in Year 5, and they're abstract in a way that whole numbers aren't. Many children can add fractions with the same denominator but come unstuck the moment denominators differ. Decimals cause a different confusion: students often treat 0.3 and 0.30 as different numbers, or rank 0.8 as smaller than 0.15 because 15 looks bigger than 8.

**Area and perimeter.** The concepts themselves aren't hard. The confusion is that they sound similar and are easily mixed up under pressure. Students regularly add all four sides when asked for area, or multiply when asked for perimeter. This isn't carelessness. It's a sign the underlying idea hasn't been tied to anything concrete yet.

**Multi-step word problems.** By Year 5, problems stop telling students which operation to use. A question might require addition, then division, before arriving at an answer. Students who've learned procedures but haven't practised reading problems carefully hit a wall here. This is also the gap that shows up most visibly on NAPLAN.

## Why Year 5 matters for NAPLAN

The Year 7 NAPLAN numeracy test draws heavily on the concepts built during Years 5 and 6. Fractions, decimals, area, and multi-step reasoning are all consistently represented. A student who leaves Year 5 shaky on those topics arrives at Year 7 NAPLAN carrying two years of unresolved confusion.

The good news is that Year 5 is early enough to fix things without pressure. There's time for proper understanding rather than cramming. A gap spotted now, at age 10 or 11, is a much easier repair than the same gap at 12 with a test six months away.

## What Year 5 maths sets up for secondary school

The primary-to-secondary transition is significant mathematically. Year 7 maths in Victoria moves quickly and assumes fluency with everything from primary school. Fractions, percentages, and basic algebra all appear in the first term. Students who arrive with gaps in Year 5 and 6 content often find Year 7 disorienting not because they're not capable, but because they're catching up and learning new material at the same time.

For strong students, Year 5 is also a chance to go further. Extension work on number patterns, negative numbers, and early algebraic thinking builds genuine advantage heading into secondary. The students who find Year 7 maths straightforward are usually the ones who were extended, not just kept on pace, during primary.

## What you can do at home right now

You don't need to reteach the curriculum. A few focused habits make a real difference.

Ask your child to explain a maths concept to you in their own words. If they can't explain it, they probably can't apply it under pressure either. Keep it low-stakes and curious rather than quizzy.

Work on fluency with fractions and decimals in everyday contexts. Cooking, splitting costs, and reading sale prices all give real meaning to abstract numbers.

If word problems are the sticking point, slow them down. Read the problem twice, identify what's being asked before touching numbers, then work through it step by step. That habit alone closes a lot of the gap.

## When a tutor helps

If you're noticing specific gaps or want your child genuinely extended beyond year level, a targeted tutor is worth considering. After each session you should know what was covered, how your child went, and what to practise next, so you're not left guessing whether the sessions are working.

Practice between sessions should match the Victorian Curriculum at your child's level, so there's no hunting around for the right worksheets.
