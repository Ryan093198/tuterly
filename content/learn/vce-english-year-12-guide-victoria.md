## What VCE English actually involves

VCE English Units 3 and 4 is a full-year subject that contributes directly to your child's ATAR. It is one of the few subjects that almost every Year 12 student in Victoria takes, yet many parents have only a vague sense of what it actually demands.

The subject is built around three areas of study. Each one has its own skills, its own assessment tasks, and its own way of tripping students up if they are not prepared. Understanding the structure from the start of the year, not just in Term 4, makes an enormous difference.

## The three areas of study

**Reading and Comparing** asks students to read two texts closely, usually a novel or memoir alongside a film or play, and write an essay that explores a common theme across both. The skill here is not summarising the texts. It is developing a genuine argument about how each author constructs meaning, and linking the two through comparative analysis.

**Creating** requires students to produce an original piece of writing in response to a prompt, along with a written explanation called a statement of intention. This area trips up a lot of students who assume that being creative means writing freely. The assessors are looking for deliberate choices about form, structure, and language. Every decision needs to be defensible.

**Analysing Argument** involves reading or viewing a persuasive piece and writing an analytical response that explains how the author attempts to position their audience. This is not about whether your child agrees with the argument. It is about identifying techniques, explaining their intended effect, and building a clear, structured analysis.

## How assessment works across the year

VCE English is assessed through a combination of School-Assessed Coursework (SACs) and an end-of-year exam. The SACs contribute 33% of the final study score and the exam contributes 67%.

SACs are set and marked by your child's school. There are typically three or four across the year, and they are completed under timed, supervised conditions. Missing a SAC without medical documentation can have serious consequences for your child's school-assessed component, so it is worth keeping track of the schedule early.

The exam runs for three hours and covers all three areas of study. Students produce three separate pieces of writing in a single sitting. Time management under that pressure is a skill in itself, and it needs practice well before October.

## Where students lose marks

The most common issues are not about intelligence or effort. They are about technique that has not been explicitly taught.

In Reading and Comparing, students often write two separate essays glued together with the word "similarly." A strong comparative essay weaves the two texts together throughout, building an argument that cannot exist without both.

In Creating, students lose marks when their statement of intention does not match what is actually on the page. Saying you intended to create suspense through sentence fragments, then not doing it, signals a gap between intention and execution.

In Analysing Argument, students tend to list techniques without explaining why the author chose them or what effect they are designed to have on a specific audience. Technique identification is only the first step. The analysis is what earns the marks.

## What high performers do differently

Students who score in the upper bands are not necessarily more talented readers or writers. They are students who have internalised the criteria and can apply them consistently under pressure.

They write with a clear argument from the first sentence. They revise their own work with a critical eye before submitting. They have practised each task type enough times that the structure feels automatic, which frees them to focus on the quality of ideas rather than remembering what to do next.

For a student aiming for a high study score, the work starts in Term 1, not Term 3. The texts for Reading and Comparing need time to be understood deeply. Rushing through them in the final weeks before the exam is one of the most common mistakes students make.

## How to support your child without becoming their editor

Your child needs to own their own writing. The moment you start rewriting their sentences, you remove the thinking the task was designed to develop.

What you can do is ask good questions. "What is your actual argument in this paragraph?" "How does this connect back to the prompt?" "Which part of this are you least confident about?" These questions prompt reflection without removing agency.

You can also help with the logistics. Keeping track of SAC dates, making sure they have quiet time to draft and revise, and noticing when they seem stuck for longer than is normal. These things matter more than most parents realise.
