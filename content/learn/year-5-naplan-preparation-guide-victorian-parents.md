Year 5 is the NAPLAN year many Victorian parents start paying proper attention. Year 3 often feels like a trial run. Year 7 and 9 feel distant. But Year 5 sits right in the middle, and for a lot of children, it is the first time their results carry weight in their own minds.

The good news: Year 5 NAPLAN is very preparable. You do not need to overhaul your child's study life or book daily tutoring sessions. You need to know what is actually being tested, where Year 5 raises the bar from Year 3, and what a few weeks of focused practice looks like at home.

## What Year 5 NAPLAN actually tests

NAPLAN tests four areas across all year levels: Reading, Writing, Conventions of Language (spelling, grammar and punctuation), and Numeracy. The content is drawn directly from the Australian Curriculum, which Victoria follows through the Victorian Curriculum F-10.

At Year 5, the expectations are meaningfully higher than Year 3. In Numeracy, students are expected to work with fractions, decimals, percentages at an introductory level, and multi-step problems. In Reading, inferential questions become more common. Students are not just finding information on the page; they are being asked what a text implies or how a writer has structured an argument.

Writing sits on a single extended task. Students are given a prompt and 42 minutes to produce a persuasive or narrative piece. Spelling, grammar and punctuation are tested separately through multiple-choice and short-answer questions.

## Where Year 5 raises the bar

The jump from Year 3 to Year 5 catches some children off guard, and it helps to know where.

In Numeracy, worded problems get longer and involve more steps. A Year 3 question might ask a child to add two numbers. A Year 5 question gives them a scenario, requires them to identify the right operation, carry it out correctly, and often convert their answer into a unit or format the question specifies. Speed matters too. The online test is timed, and students who pause on unfamiliar question types can run short at the end.

In Reading, the texts are longer and the question types more layered. Students who read widely and often tend to handle this section well. Students who mostly read at a single level, or who have not been pushed to discuss what they read, often find the inference questions harder than expected.

## What you can do at home right now

You do not need expensive resources or a rigid schedule. A few consistent habits in the weeks before the test will do more than a fortnight of cramming.

**Read together and ask questions.** After your child reads anything, a chapter book, a news article, a short story, ask them what the author was trying to show, not just what happened. That habit directly builds the inference skills NAPLAN tests.

**Bring maths into real life.** Cooking, shopping, and building things all involve the fractions, measurement, and problem-solving that appear in Year 5 Numeracy. Real contexts make abstract concepts land faster than worksheets alone.

**Write regularly and for a purpose.** Ask your child to write a short argument about something they genuinely care about: why their favourite sport is the best, whether schools should have homework. Practising the structure of persuasive writing before test day removes a lot of the freeze-up that happens under time pressure.

**Do a practice run under test conditions.** Sit your child down with a past-style paper, set a timer, and let them work through it without help. Not to judge the result, but so the format feels familiar. A child who has never written to a timer before finds the experience itself distracting on test day.

## A note for parents of stronger students

If your child is already working above year level, NAPLAN preparation is still worthwhile, but the goal shifts. The aim is not to catch up; it is to be precise and fast under pressure, and to avoid the careless errors that cost marks on familiar material.

Extension students often drop points in Writing, not because they cannot write well, but because they use the test as a chance to be creative without attending to structure. Practising the specific conventions of a persuasive or narrative response, clear orientation, developed body, strong conclusion, will sharpen what is already there.

## The week before the test

Keep it calm. A big push in the final days rarely helps and can knock confidence just when you want it steady. Make sure your child knows what to expect: an online test, timed sections, and the fact that some questions are meant to be hard. Knowing that feeling stuck on a question is normal, not a sign of failure, reduces the anxiety that costs performance more than any knowledge gap.

A good night's sleep and a proper breakfast matter more in that final week than any extra worksheet.
