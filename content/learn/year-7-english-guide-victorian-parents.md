## The Year 7 English jump is bigger than most parents expect

The move from primary to secondary school brings a lot of changes, but the shift in English is one that catches many families off guard. In primary school, writing usually means stories. Personal recounts, creative narratives, maybe a persuasive letter or two. By the end of Year 7, your child is expected to analyse texts, write structured arguments, and discuss how authors make deliberate choices to influence readers.

That is a different skill set entirely, and not every child arrives ready for it.

## What Year 7 English actually covers

Under the Victorian Curriculum F–10 Version 2.0, Year 7 English is built around three strands: Language, Literature, and Literacy. In practice, this means your child's teacher is working across reading comprehension, writing across multiple genres, speaking and listening, and close analysis of how texts are constructed.

The genres your child will encounter go well beyond fiction. Informational texts, persuasive writing, poetry, and media texts all feature. The expectation is that students can move between reading and producing these forms, not just recognise them.

## Where students typically struggle

The biggest sticking point for most Year 7 students is the jump from writing stories to writing analytically. Identifying a theme in a novel is one thing. Writing a structured paragraph that argues a point about a text, using evidence and explaining why that evidence matters, is quite another.

Many students also find the volume of reading harder than expected. Secondary English texts are longer and less familiar than primary readers, and inferring meaning from a complex passage takes practice. For students who found primary school reading manageable but never stretched themselves, the Year 7 workload can come as a shock.

## What good looks like by the end of Year 7

By the time your child finishes Year 7, the Victorian Curriculum expects them to read and view a range of texts for different purposes, write in several forms with growing control over structure and language choices, and discuss how texts position readers.

In plain terms, a student on track by end of Year 7 can write a structured analytical paragraph using the TEEL or similar format, use evidence from a text to support an argument, adapt their writing style to suit purpose and audience, and discuss language features like metaphor, tone, or point of view with some confidence.

If your child is doing well, they should be doing these things fluently, not just adequately. High-achieving students at this level begin to develop their own voice in analytical writing and engage critically with texts, not just describe them.

## How to support your child at home

You do not need to be an English teacher to help. The most useful thing you can do is talk about what your child is reading. Ask what the author might have wanted readers to feel, or why a character made a particular choice. These conversations build the interpretive thinking that Year 7 English rewards.

If your child is struggling with essay structure, work through one paragraph together rather than the whole task. Identifying a point, finding a quote, and explaining it in their own words is a repeatable process. Once it clicks, they can apply it independently.

For students who are already strong readers and writers, the focus should be depth rather than volume. Can they explain not just what a text says, but how it creates that effect? Pushing into more complex texts and more sophisticated arguments is where extension students make real gains.

## When to bring in extra support

If your child is finding analytical writing confusing after a term, or if report comments keep mentioning essay structure or text response, that is a reasonable point to seek help. A good tutor will not just explain the format once. They will work through real texts with your child, give feedback on drafts, and build the habit of thinking analytically.

For students who are thriving and want to stay ahead, a tutor experienced in Victorian secondary English can introduce more challenging texts, practise extended responses, and sharpen the kind of writing that scores well in later years.
