Year 5 is the year English quietly gets harder. The jump from Year 4 is not always obvious from the outside, but inside the classroom the Victorian Curriculum shifts its expectations in ways that catch a lot of students off guard. If your child has sailed through primary school English but is starting to struggle, or if they are doing fine but you want to make sure they are being stretched, this is worth understanding.

## What the Victorian Curriculum expects in Year 5 English

The Victorian Curriculum F-10 organises English into three strands: Language, Literature, and Literacy. By Year 5, all three are asking for more than most parents realise.

In writing, students are expected to plan, draft, and revise their work independently. That means thinking about audience and purpose before they start, not just telling a story in the order it happened. Persuasive writing becomes a serious focus, and students need to construct an argument with a clear point of view, supporting reasons, and some awareness that a reader might disagree.

In reading, the expectation is not just comprehension but inference. Students should be drawing conclusions the text does not spell out, identifying how an author's word choices shape meaning, and comparing ideas across more than one text. That is a significant cognitive step up from Year 4.

In speaking and listening, students are expected to contribute to structured discussions, present ideas clearly, and respond to others' points rather than just waiting for their turn.

## What typically trips students up

The shift to persuasive writing is the most common sticking point. Many Year 5 students write a recount dressed up as an argument: "I think dogs are great pets. I have a dog. My dog is fluffy." They are stating opinions without building a case, and without understanding why that is a different skill.

Inference in reading is the other one. A student who reads well at the literal level can still struggle when a question asks them to explain why a character made a particular choice, or what a phrase suggests about the setting. These questions reward thinking about text, not just reading it, and that is a habit that needs deliberate practice.

For students who are strong readers and writers, the gap between what the curriculum requires and what they are capable of can go the other way. Without extension tasks that push structure, vocabulary, and complexity, capable Year 5 writers often plateau when they should be accelerating.

## What to watch for term by term

Victorian schools typically follow a similar broad rhythm through the year, though the exact sequence varies by school.

Early in the year, most classes focus on narrative writing and reading fiction texts closely. If your child is struggling to build a complete story with a beginning, a problem, and a resolution, that is worth addressing now before persuasive writing arrives.

By mid-year, persuasive and informational texts become central. This is where many parents first notice that their child can write fluently but struggles to organise ideas logically. If your child's writing reads like a list of thoughts rather than a structured argument, that is a specific skill gap, not a general English problem.

Later in the year, grammar and punctuation expectations sharpen. Year 5 students should understand how to use complex sentences, punctuate dialogue correctly, and vary their sentence structure for effect. Students who have been getting by on good ideas without technical precision start to show up in their results.

## What you can do at home

Reading together is still valuable in Year 5, but the conversation around the reading matters more than it did in earlier years. After a chapter, ask your child what they think a character was feeling, and why. Ask them to find the sentence that tells them that. You are building inference, not quizzing comprehension.

For writing, the most useful thing you can do is ask your child to explain their plan before they start writing. Who is the audience? What do they want that person to think or feel by the end? Students who can answer those questions tend to produce more purposeful writing than students who just begin.

Vocabulary is often the unspoken factor in Year 5 English results. Students with a wider reading diet tend to write with more precision. Encouraging your child to read across genres, not just their preferred series, makes a measurable difference over a year.

If your child is a strong writer, give them harder problems: write a persuasive letter arguing both sides, retell a story from a secondary character's point of view, or rewrite a news article as a narrative. These tasks develop the flexibility that separates a good Year 5 writer from a genuinely impressive one.

## When to bring in more support

If your child is avoiding writing tasks at home, producing very short work compared to what the teacher asks for, or consistently getting feedback about organisation and structure, those are signs the gap is widening rather than closing. Year 5 is the right time to address it, well before the NAPLAN Year 5 assessment and the more demanding expectations of upper primary.

For students performing well but not being stretched, a tutor who works on extension tasks, rather than just reinforcing what school already covers, is worth finding specifically.

A good primary English tutor understands the Victorian Curriculum and can pinpoint exactly which strand is causing difficulty. Ask them to tell you after each session what was worked on and what your child should practise before the next one, so the work between sessions has clear direction rather than relying on random worksheets.
