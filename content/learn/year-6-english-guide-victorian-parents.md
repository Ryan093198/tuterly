The jump from primary to secondary school is one of the biggest academic shifts your child will make. In English, it happens fast. Year 7 teachers assume students arrive with a solid set of literacy skills, and when those skills have gaps, it shows up quickly in the first semester.

Here is what the Victorian Curriculum actually expects at Level 6, where the gaps tend to hide, and what you can do about it before February.

## What the Victorian Curriculum expects at Level 6

Under the Victorian Curriculum F–10, Level 6 English covers three strands: Language, Literature, and Literacy. By the end of Year 6, students are expected to understand how language choices shape meaning, read and respond to a range of texts including complex narratives and informational writing, and produce their own structured writing across multiple genres.

In practice, that means your child should be able to do a few specific things with confidence. They should analyse why an author made a particular word choice, not just describe what happened in the story. They should write a narrative with a clear structure, developed characters, and varied sentence construction. And they should read a passage they have never seen before and answer questions about both literal meaning and implied meaning.

## The skills that most often have gaps

**Reading comprehension, particularly inference.** Literal comprehension is usually fine by Year 6. The gap tends to sit in inference: reading between the lines, identifying a character's motivation, or understanding how a text creates a particular effect on the reader. Year 7 English moves quickly into this territory, and students who have only ever answered "what happened" questions struggle when teachers start asking "why" and "how."

**Grammar terminology.** Your child may write perfectly well but have no idea what a subordinate clause is. This matters more than it sounds, because Year 7 teachers use grammatical metalanguage to give feedback. If your child cannot identify a verb phrase or an adverbial, written feedback becomes harder to act on.

**Persuasive and analytical writing.** Most Year 6 students have written a persuasive text. Fewer have learned to structure an argument with a clear contention, supporting evidence, and a response to a counterpoint. This is foundational for Year 7 and grows significantly in Years 8 and 9, so arriving without it puts students behind from the start.

**Editing their own work.** Writing a first draft is not the same as improving it. Year 6 students who have always submitted first drafts without a real revision process often find secondary school marking confronting, because teachers mark the final version and expect it to reflect deliberate choices.

## A note for strong readers and writers

If your child reads widely and writes confidently, Year 6 is a good time to stretch rather than consolidate. Extension at this stage looks different from remediation.

Strong students benefit from exposure to more complex texts, including older literature, literary non-fiction, and texts that challenge their assumptions. They also benefit from learning to write analytically rather than just creatively. If your child loves storytelling, introducing them to the idea that a writer makes deliberate structural choices, and that those choices can be named and discussed, opens up a deeper relationship with English that serves them all the way through secondary school.

## What you can do at home

Read together and ask genuine questions about the text. Not comprehension quiz questions, but real ones: "Why do you think the author ended the chapter there?" or "What kind of person do you think this character is, and how do you know?"

Encourage a second draft habit. When your child finishes a piece of writing, ask them to read it aloud. Most students hear their own errors more clearly than they see them.

Talk about grammar in low-stakes moments. You do not need to run formal lessons. Pointing out a well-constructed sentence in a book your child is reading, or asking "what's the verb in that sentence?" over dinner, builds familiarity without pressure.
