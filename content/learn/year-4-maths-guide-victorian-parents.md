Year 4 is one of those years that looks manageable from the outside. No NAPLAN sitting, no big transition, no dramatic milestone. But inside the Victorian curriculum, it is one of the most loaded years in primary maths. The concepts that land in Year 4 either click and carry your child forward, or quietly set up the struggles you will be untangling in Year 6 and beyond.

This guide covers what your child is actually meant to know by the end of Year 4, where the gaps tend to form, and what you can do about it at home.

## What the Victorian curriculum expects in Year 4 maths

The Victorian Curriculum F-10 sets clear expectations for Year 4 across three big areas: number and algebra, measurement and geometry, and statistics and probability.

In practice, the ones that matter most for later learning are multiplication and division facts, place value into the thousands, and the beginnings of fractions and decimals. If your child is solid on those three, the rest of Year 4 maths tends to follow.

## Multiplication: the one that causes the most grief later

By the end of Year 4, children are expected to recall multiplication facts up to 10 x 10, and to use those facts fluently in written and mental strategies. This is not about worksheets and rote chanting. Fluency means your child can retrieve 7 x 8 without working it out from scratch, because they will need that mental space free for more complex problems in Year 5 and beyond.

The gap shows up in a specific way. A child who never fully automated their times tables reaches Year 5 long multiplication and spends so much effort retrieving basic facts that they lose track of the method. That is when parents notice the problem, but it started here.

If your child is shaky on any tables beyond 2, 5, and 10, that is worth addressing before Year 5 starts.

## Place value to the thousands

Year 4 students work with numbers up to at least four digits, reading, writing, ordering, and rounding them. They also start to understand how the place of a digit changes its value, not just as a rule to memorise but as something to apply in addition, subtraction, and estimation.

A concrete check: ask your child to tell you the value of each digit in 3,724. If they can do that quickly and explain why the 7 represents 700 rather than 7, they have the concept. If they hesitate or confuse place with face value, that is a genuine gap worth working on.

## Fractions: where confidence often cracks first

Year 4 introduces fractions including halves, quarters, thirds, and eighths. Students are expected to compare and order fractions with the same denominator, and to find fractions of collections and shapes.

This is where you start seeing the split between children who find maths intuitive and those who are working harder to keep up. The abstract nature of fractions catches many Year 4 students off guard, especially when the visual models disappear and they are expected to work with numerals alone.

Using physical objects at home helps more than most parents expect. Cutting an apple into quarters, splitting a set of 12 blocks into thirds, asking which is bigger: two-eighths or one-quarter. These are not baby activities. They are how the concept actually sticks.

## For children who are already ahead

If your child has the core content under control, Year 4 is a good year to extend rather than accelerate. Extending means going deeper into the same concepts rather than rushing to Year 5 material.

That might look like exploring why multiplication is commutative, working through multi-step word problems that combine operations, or investigating patterns in multiplication tables. These build the mathematical reasoning skills that separate students who cope with hard problems from students who genuinely enjoy them. Both NAPLAN in Year 5 and the extension work in Year 7 maths reward exactly this kind of flexible thinking.

## Signs a gap is forming

A few things worth watching for, without catastrophising:

- Avoidance or anxiety around maths homework that was not there in Year 3
- Slow or inconsistent recall of times tables past the 2s, 5s, and 10s
- Difficulty explaining answers, even when they get the right number
- Confusion when questions use slightly different wording than they are used to

Any one of these on its own is not cause for alarm. All of them together, or any of them persisting past a month of gentle practice at home, is worth taking seriously.

## How to help at home without turning dinner into a maths lesson

Little and often works better than long sessions. Ten minutes of times table practice three times a week beats an hour on Sunday. Vary the format: verbal questions, written drills, card games, and apps all reinforce different aspects of fluency.

Ask your child to explain their working out to you, even if you can already see the answer. The act of explaining forces a child to organise their thinking, and you will quickly hear whether the understanding is there or whether they have memorised a procedure without grasping the concept.

## When home support is not quite enough

If the gaps feel persistent or your child is starting to disengage from maths, that is a reasonable moment to bring in some targeted support. A good tutor does not reteach the whole year.
