Linear graphs sit right in the middle of Year 9 maths in the Victorian Curriculum 2.0, and they cause a surprising amount of grief. Not because the ideas are complicated, but because students pick up small habits early that quietly cost them marks on every question from then on.

If your child just came home with a disappointing result on a linear graphs test, or is heading into one soon, this is worth reading. The mistakes below are predictable and fixable.

## Why linear graphs matter beyond Year 9

This topic does not stop at the end of the unit. Gradient and intercept reappear in Year 10 when students work with linear relationships and simultaneous equations, and again in VCE Maths Methods from the first week. A shaky foundation here tends to follow students for years.

The good news is that most of the errors come from a small number of misunderstood ideas, not from the topic being inherently difficult.

## Mistake 1: mixing up rise and run

The gradient formula is rise over run, but students regularly flip it. They calculate run over rise and get a reciprocal that looks plausible, so it passes without a second glance.

The fix is to practise reading the formula out loud before every calculation: how far up (or down), divided by how far across. Drawing the triangle on the graph and labelling rise and run before touching the calculator helps more than drilling the formula from memory.

## Mistake 2: getting the sign of the gradient wrong

A line going downward from left to right has a negative gradient. Many students know this in theory but write a positive value anyway, because they count the rise and run as separate positive numbers and then forget to apply the direction.

The check is simple: if the line falls as you move right, the gradient must be negative. Building that as a habit, checking the sign against the direction of the line before writing the answer, catches this every time.

## Mistake 3: confusing gradient and y-intercept in y = mx + b

When students see y = 3x + 5, most can tell you the gradient is 3 and the y-intercept is 5. But rearrange the equation slightly, say to y = 5 + 3x, or y = 3x, or y = -2x - 4, and the error rate jumps.

The common confusion is reading the first number after the equals sign as the gradient regardless of which term it is. Practising with equations in non-standard order, and always identifying which term contains x before reading off the gradient, is the most direct fix.

## Mistake 4: misreading the y-intercept from a graph

The y-intercept is where the line crosses the y-axis. Students sometimes read the x-intercept by accident, especially when the graph is small or the line crosses close to the origin. Others correctly identify the crossing point but write the x-coordinate instead of the y-coordinate.

A reliable habit: when you find the intercept on a graph, name the point as a coordinate pair first, then pick out the y-value. That extra step interrupts the rush.

## Mistake 5: finding the equation of a line given two points

This is usually the hardest question on a linear graphs test, and the place where multiple small errors can compound. Students might calculate the gradient correctly but then substitute the wrong point when finding b. Or they substitute correctly but make a sign error rearranging for b.

The clearest method is to work in steps, writing each one on a separate line: find the gradient, substitute one point and m into y = mx + b, then solve for b on its own line. When everything is on one line, sign errors hide easily.

## Mistake 6: parallel and perpendicular lines

Parallel lines have the same gradient. Perpendicular lines have gradients that multiply to give negative one. Both ideas are covered in the Victorian Curriculum at Year 9, and both appear regularly in tests.

The perpendicular rule catches students out because "flipping the fraction and changing the sign" is easy to remember but easy to apply only halfway. A student who flips the fraction but forgets to change the sign, or vice versa, will get a wrong answer that looks structured enough to feel correct.

Always check: multiply the two gradients. If they don't give exactly negative one, something went wrong.

## What to do in the week before a test

Understanding these mistakes is a start, but reading about them is not the same as being able to do the question under test conditions.

The approach that tends to work is practising questions on each of these error types specifically, checking the answer, understanding exactly which step went wrong, then doing a fresh question on the same idea. Not the same question again. A new one, different numbers, different setup, so that the method has to be applied rather than the answer remembered.

If your child can answer a fresh gradient question correctly three days after they last practised it, it is in long-term memory. If they can only do it right immediately after studying it, it probably is not.

## High-performing students: where to push further

If your child already has the basics and wants to extend their understanding before Year 10, there is plenty of room to go deeper. Finding the equation of a line from a graph without a grid, working with lines expressed in general form (ax + by = c), and exploring why perpendicular gradients multiply to negative one (not just memorising that they do) are all worthwhile.

The Victorian Curriculum 2.0 includes working with linear relationships in context, applying them to real-world situations and interpreting what gradient and intercept actually mean in those contexts. These are the questions that separate a solid result from an outstanding one.
