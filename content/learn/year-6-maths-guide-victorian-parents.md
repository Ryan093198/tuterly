## Why Year 6 maths matters more than most parents realise

The jump from primary to secondary school is one of the biggest transitions your child will make. In maths, it is not just a change of school or teacher. It is a shift in pace, expectation, and abstraction. Year 7 teachers generally assume students arrive with solid foundations. When those foundations have gaps, children fall behind quickly and quietly, often before anyone notices.

Year 6 is the year to sort this out. Not in a stressful, exam-prep way. Just by being clear on what your child actually knows, what they do not, and how to close the gap before September.

## What the Victorian Curriculum expects in Year 6

Under the Victorian Curriculum F–10, Year 6 maths covers a lot of ground. The key areas to know:

**Number and algebra.** This is the big one. Students are expected to work confidently with fractions, decimals, and percentages, including converting between them. They should be able to multiply and divide fractions, solve problems involving ratios and rates, and apply the four operations to integers. Order of operations also appears here, which trips up a surprising number of students heading into Year 7.

**Measurement and geometry.** Area, perimeter, and volume of common shapes. Angles in triangles and quadrilaterals. Coordinates on the Cartesian plane. These topics show up immediately in Year 7, so shaky understanding here tends to compound.

**Statistics and probability.** Interpreting data from tables and graphs, calculating mean, median, and mode, and understanding simple probability. These are often treated as the "easy" topics, but the language-heavy questions catch many students off guard.

## The gaps that follow kids into Year 7

After working with families across Melbourne, a few gaps come up again and again.

**Fractions.** Many students can add simple fractions but struggle when denominators are different, or when they need to multiply or divide. Year 7 algebra builds directly on this.

**Place value and decimals.** Students who are shaky on place value tend to make consistent errors with decimals that compound into larger problems in secondary maths.

**Order of operations.** BODMAS (or BIDMAS, as some schools teach it) is introduced in upper primary but rarely drilled enough. Year 7 teachers expect it to be automatic.

**Worded problems.** This is less about maths and more about reading carefully and setting up the problem. It is worth practising regularly, because NAPLAN and secondary school assessments lean heavily on this format.

## For children who are already doing well

Extension in Year 6 is worth taking seriously, especially if your child finds the standard curriculum straightforward. Students who are ahead have a real opportunity to get comfortable with pre-algebra concepts: using unknowns, understanding variables, and working with patterns and sequences. These are not Year 7 topics being pushed early for the sake of it. They are the ideas that separate students who hit Year 7 with confidence from those who have to catch up.

If your child is interested in selective entry or scholarship exams, Year 6 is also the year to start building reasoning skills, not just content knowledge.

## What actually helps

Worksheets have their place, but passive repetition of the same type of question rarely moves the needle. What works is spaced practice across mixed topics, short enough to stay focused, regular enough to build fluency.

A few practical things you can do at home:

- Ask your child to explain how they got an answer, not just what the answer is. If they cannot explain it, they have probably memorised a method rather than understood it.
- Watch for avoidance. Children who "hate maths" are usually struggling with one or two specific things, not maths as a whole.
- Make sure they can work without a calculator on the core operations. Year 7 maths moves faster when mental arithmetic is automatic.
