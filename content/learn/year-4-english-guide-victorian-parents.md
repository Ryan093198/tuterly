Year 4 is a quiet turning point in English, and it catches a lot of families off guard. Up until this point, your child was learning to read. From Year 4 onwards, the Victorian Curriculum expects them to read to learn. That shift changes everything, and if a child's foundations are shaky, the gap between them and their peers widens quickly and quietly.

The first visible signal for many families is a Year 5 NAPLAN result that feels like it came from nowhere. It didn't. The seeds were usually sitting in Year 4.

## What the Victorian Curriculum actually expects in Year 4

The Victorian Curriculum F-10 sets clear expectations for Year 4 English across three strands: Language, Literature, and Literacy. In plain terms, that means your child is expected to:

- Read with fluency and understand why a text is structured the way it is, not just what it says
- Write in a range of forms including narratives, information texts, and simple persuasive pieces
- Use correct spelling for common and topic-specific words, and apply consistent grammar and punctuation
- Discuss vocabulary choices, including how authors use language to create an effect

Comprehension at this level is no longer about finding the right sentence. It involves inference, identifying the main idea across a whole passage, and explaining a character's motivation using evidence from the text.

## The three things most worth watching

**Reading comprehension.** This is where the gap tends to open first. A child who reads fluently but answers comprehension questions by scanning for keywords will start to struggle when texts become longer and questions more inferential. Watch for a child who reads confidently but can't explain what they just read in their own words.

**Spelling and vocabulary.** Year 4 spelling moves well beyond sight words into morphology, meaning word parts like prefixes, suffixes, and roots. Children who are still memorising words one at a time rather than understanding patterns will find this load increases faster than they can keep up.

**Written expression.** Year 4 students are expected to plan, draft, and improve their writing, not just produce a first attempt. If your child's writing is consistently short, avoids detail, or starts every sentence the same way, that's worth addressing now rather than in Year 5.

## What you can do at home, practically

Reading together is still worthwhile at this age, even though many parents assume it's only for younger children. The difference is in the conversation afterwards. Ask your child what they think a character was feeling and why. Ask them what the author wanted them to think about. These questions build the inferential thinking that comprehension tasks at school demand.

For spelling, short and regular beats long and occasional. Ten minutes three times a week looking at word families (words built from the same root) is more effective than a single half-hour session before a test. The Victorian Curriculum specifically emphasises spelling strategies over rote memorisation, so help your child notice patterns rather than just rehearse lists.

For writing, the single most useful habit is asking your child to re-read one sentence and see if they can make it more interesting. This is exactly the kind of revision skill the curriculum asks students to develop, and it doesn't require you to be an English teacher.

## High performers need stretching too

If your child is already meeting Year 4 expectations comfortably, that's worth building on rather than coasting through. Extension at this level looks like reading longer and more complex texts, experimenting with different writing styles, and developing a genuine reading habit across different genres including non-fiction. Children who are stretched in Year 4 tend to handle the jump to Year 5 comprehension tasks with much more confidence.

## When to bring in extra support

If your child is avoiding reading, struggling to complete written tasks at school, or coming home confused about spelling patterns, acting early is much easier than catching up later. Year 4 is early enough that targeted support makes a real difference before NAPLAN preparation becomes the focus.

Look for a tutor who works to the Victorian Curriculum and focuses on the specific skills your child actually needs, whether that's building comprehension strategies, strengthening writing structure, or extending a strong student further. Ask them to tell you after each session what was covered and what to practise next, so the work continues between sessions.
