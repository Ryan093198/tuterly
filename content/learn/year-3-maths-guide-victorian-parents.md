Year 3 is the year maths quietly gets harder. Not dramatically, not all at once, but the shift is real. Children move from counting and grouping things they can touch to working with numbers and relationships they have to hold in their heads. For some kids this clicks naturally. For others, Year 3 is where a small wobble turns into a lasting gap.

Knowing what your child is working on, and where the common sticking points are, puts you in a far better position to help.

## What the Victorian Curriculum covers in Year 3 maths

The Victorian Curriculum F-10 organises Year 3 maths into two main strands: Number and Algebra, and Measurement and Space. The bulk of parent concern sits in Number, so that is where this guide focuses.

By the end of Year 3, children are expected to:

- Understand and work with numbers up to at least 10,000 (four-digit place value)
- Recall multiplication facts for 2, 3, 5, and 10
- Connect multiplication and division as related operations
- Work with simple fractions, including halves, quarters, and eighths
- Add and subtract two and three-digit numbers using efficient strategies

That is a significant jump from Year 2, where the focus was on numbers to 1,000 and early skip-counting.

## The four topics where children most often get stuck

**Place value to four digits.** Moving from three-digit to four-digit numbers seems small but trips many children up. The issue is usually the thousands column, specifically understanding that 4,200 and 4,020 are different numbers. If your child is muddling large numbers, ten minutes with a whiteboard and some bundling exercises (groups of ten hundreds make a thousand) goes a long way.

**Multiplication facts.** The curriculum expects Year 3 students to be building fluency, not just understanding the concept. Slow recall makes everything else harder: mental arithmetic, division, and later, fractions. Little and often works best here. Three minutes of mixed fact practice before school beats a weekly worksheet session.

**The link between multiplication and division.** Children often treat these as separate things to memorise. The Victorian Curriculum explicitly teaches them as inverse operations, so if your child knows 5 x 4 = 20, they should also be able to work out 20 divided by 4 without starting from scratch. If this connection is not landing, try working backwards from a multiplication fact rather than presenting division as its own new thing.

**Fractions.** Year 3 introduces the idea that fractions are equal parts of a whole. The word "equal" is doing a lot of work there, and many children skip over it. Folding paper into quarters is worth ten times more than a worksheet at this stage. Concrete first, then diagrams, then numbers.

## Practical things you can do at home

You do not need to recreate school at your kitchen table. Small, consistent habits matter far more than long study sessions.

- Ask your child to explain what they did in maths that day. If they can explain it back to you simply, they probably understand it. If they cannot, that is useful information.
- Bring maths into everyday life without making it feel like homework. Sharing food into equal portions, reading prices at the supermarket, and checking change are all genuine Year 3 maths in context.
- When your child is stuck, resist the urge to show them the answer. Ask "what do you already know about this?" first. That habit builds the independent problem-solving the curriculum is aiming for.
- If they are finding it easy, push further. Ask what happens if you have 12 things and want to share them into groups of 3, then 4, then 6. Extension at home is genuinely valuable, not just catch-up.

## Signs your child might need more support

Most Year 3 children hit a rough patch at some point. That is normal and not cause for alarm. Watch out for a pattern rather than a single bad day.

Consistent avoidance of maths homework, not being able to recall any multiplication facts by mid-year, or real distress around number work are worth taking seriously. So is the opposite: a child who races through everything and is disengaged because nothing is challenging enough.

## What to do next

If you are at the "so what do I actually do?" point, start with short, regular practice on the specific Year 3 topic that is causing trouble. If that is not enough, a tutor who knows the Victorian Curriculum and works with Year 3 children regularly can help. Ask them to tell you after each session what was covered and what to practise before next time, so there is no guesswork about what to focus on.
