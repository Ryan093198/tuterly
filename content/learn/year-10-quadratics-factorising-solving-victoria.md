Quadratics are the moment Year 10 maths stops feeling like an extension of Year 9 and starts feeling genuinely hard. Most students can follow along in class, but when a test arrives, the method they think they know quietly falls apart.

This matters beyond the grade. The Victorian Curriculum 2.0 builds factorising and solving quadratics into Year 10, and VCE Maths Methods picks up exactly where that leaves off. A shaky foundation now costs real marks later.

## Why guessing is not a method

The classic approach most students land on is this: look at the constant, think of two numbers that multiply to give it, check if they add to the middle term, adjust, repeat. For simple cases it works. For anything harder, it wastes time and produces errors that look random but are not.

The problem is not that students are careless. It is that they never learned a reliable process. They learned the idea of factorising. That is different.

## The four mistakes that show up most in Victorian Year 10

### 1. Getting the signs wrong

A quadratic like x² + 5x + 6 is forgiving: both numbers are positive, so the sign choices are obvious. The trouble starts with x² + x − 6 or x² − 5x + 6, where students pick the right numbers but attach the wrong signs.

The fix is to build the habit of checking by expanding. If (x + 3)(x − 2) expands back to the original, the signs are right. This takes ten seconds and catches most sign errors before they cost marks.

### 2. Forgetting the null factor law

Factorising and solving are two different steps. A student might correctly write (x + 3)(x − 2) and then write x = 3 and x = 2 without understanding why. They have reversed the signs by habit, not by reasoning.

The null factor law says: if two things multiply to zero, at least one of them must be zero. So (x + 3) = 0 gives x = −3, and (x − 2) = 0 gives x = 2. Students who have not genuinely understood this get the solutions right half the time and wrong the other half, with no way to tell which is which.

### 3. Mishandling non-monic quadratics

Monic quadratics have 1 as the coefficient of x². Non-monic ones look like 2x² + 7x + 3 or 3x² − x − 10. These are on the Victorian Year 10 curriculum and they appear regularly in Maths Methods.

The trial-and-error method breaks down here because there are more combinations to test. Students who have only practised monic cases freeze, guess, or abandon the question. Learning a systematic approach, whether that is factoring by grouping or a structured method for splitting the middle term, makes these questions just as approachable as the monic ones.

### 4. Not recognising the form before starting

Some quadratics are difference of two squares (x² − 9, for example). Some are perfect square trinomials. Some need a common factor taken out first. Students who charge straight into finding factor pairs often do far more work than necessary, or choose the wrong method entirely.

A quick check at the start, before writing anything, saves time and prevents method errors. Can I take out a common factor? Is this a difference of two squares? Is the coefficient of x² equal to 1? Three questions, answered in a few seconds.

## What mastery actually looks like

A student who has genuinely mastered Year 10 quadratics can do all of the following reliably, not just on a good day:

- Identify the right method before starting
- Factorise both monic and non-monic trinomials without trial and error
- Apply the null factor law correctly every time, including with negative solutions
- Expand their answer to check it
- Recognise special forms like difference of two squares

They should also be comfortable with quadratics that do not factorise neatly and know when to use the quadratic formula instead. That is part of the Victorian Year 10 curriculum too, and it comes up in Methods almost immediately.

## For students who are ahead

If your child already has the standard cases under control, the extension work in Year 10 is worth pursuing. This includes solving quadratic equations that require rearranging first, working with quadratics in context (area problems, projectile motion), and understanding what the discriminant tells you about solutions before you solve anything.

These topics bridge directly into Unit 1 and 2 Maths Methods. Students who have seen them in Year 10 spend their early VCE time consolidating rather than catching up.

## A note for parents

You do not need to know how to factorise a non-monic quadratic to help your child. What you can do is ask them to explain their method out loud. If the explanation is "I just tried numbers until one worked", that is a sign the method is not solid. A student who can walk through the steps in words understands it. One who cannot usually does not, even if they got the right answer.

A quick way to test genuine understanding: ask them to do a question where the constant is negative. That is where the guessing approach breaks down most reliably.
